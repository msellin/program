# Naming — Round 2, Agent 2: Sharpen-Family Expansion

Angle: **explore the semantic neighbourhood of Sharpen aggressively.** Refining
verbs, precision-tuning verbs, sharpen synonyms, athletic imperatives, tactile
craft verbs, Nordic/Anglo-Saxon monosyllables. The founder likes Sharpen because
it's the direct English of Terav, transparent, verb-forward, and every product
CTA is already built on it. My job is to find a name in that family that either
beats it or complements it.

Every candidate is stress-tested against:

1. **Head-to-head with Sharpen** — does it win, and on which axis?
2. **CTA test** — "[Name] the plan tonight" reads how?
3. **Two-syllable penalty** — if it's not shorter than Sharpen, it has to win on
   metaphor, monogram, or memorability. Otherwise Sharpen wins by transparency.
4. **Insider vocabulary** — is this a phrase CrossFitters, lifters and coaches
   already use? ("dial in," "lock in," "sharpen up," "prime the pump," "level
   up" are all real gym-floor English.)
5. **Trademark clean-slate** — obviously blocked in fitness / training / mobile?

---

## Candidates

### 1. Dial (verb form of "dial in")

- **Origin.** English idiom "dial it in" — CrossFit, motorsport, audio
  engineering. To dial in is to make small, precise adjustments until the
  setting is exactly right for the job in front of you.
- **Why it fits.** This is the single most-used precision phrase on the gym
  floor. Coaches say "dial in your setup" a hundred times a week. The verb
  already carries "small corrections, not a rebuild" — exactly what the
  adaptive engine does. Monosyllable, plosive close on /l/, first-syllable
  stress. Confirms the "confirm-first" philosophy (the engine dials, the user
  confirms).
- **Why it might not.** "Dial" alone (without "in") is bland — a phone-era
  noun. The idiom is "dial in," which is two words. Also very common
  supplement / fitness product word — "Dialed In" is a Rockstar Energy line and
  the phrase is on a thousand hats.
- **CTA test.** "Dial the plan tonight." Reads a shade off — coaches say "dial
  in the plan," not "dial the plan." Full idiom wins: **"Dial in tonight's
  session."** That reads clean.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** Loses on transparency (Sharpen IS the promise;
  Dial requires the "in" preposition to complete the idiom) but wins on
  insider status by a mile.
- **Availability read.** dial.com dead (utilities/soap brand). dial.fit /
  dialin.app plausible. Crowded semantic space.

### 2. Tune

- **Origin.** English/Latin — to bring an instrument or engine to its ideal
  state. Native to lifting: "tempo work," "tune your setup," "tune the lift."
- **Why it fits.** Monosyllable, front-vowel /uː/, clean /n/ close. Precise
  metaphor — tuning isn't rebuilding, it's tightening the last increment. The
  confirm-first engine literally proposes tunings the user accepts.
- **Why it might not.** iTunes owned the word for a generation; TuneIn, dozens
  of SaaS. "Tune" also means "a song" — the noun collides with the verb. And
  the audience mostly uses "tune" for cars, not lifts.
- **CTA test.** "Tune the plan tonight." Reads clean, feels slightly musical.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** Loses. Same syllable-shape penalty (short but
  ambiguous), and Sharpen has zero noun collision in the fitness context.
- **Availability read.** Catastrophic. Every domain gone.

### 3. Hone

- **Origin.** Old English *hān* — the fine-grit stone used for the final pass
  on a blade. The refinement step *after* grinding.
- **Why it fits.** The tightest semantic match to Sharpen — same physical
  reference, same "small deliberate correction," but one syllable instead of
  two. Already in the lifter's mouth ("hone your technique"). Round 1 picked
  this in every top-3 and two of three agents made it their cheque pick.
- **Why it might not.** Hone Health (men's hormones) and Hone HQ (executive
  coaching) both own real airspace. Category-adjacent enough to worry a
  trademark lawyer.
- **CTA test.** "Hone the plan tonight." Cleaner than Sharpen — one syllable,
  one action.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Beats Sharpen on brevity and cadence.**
  Loses on transparency to a non-lifter reader (Sharpen is universally
  understood; Hone is one register up). The tie-breaker is the audience — the
  target user knows "hone" already.
- **Availability read.** hone.com blocked. hone.fit / hone.app / usehone
  plausible.

### 4. Whet

- **Origin.** Old English *hwettan* — to sharpen; the coarser pass before
  honing. Survives in "whet your appetite."
- **Why it fits.** Anglo-Saxon monosyllable, closes hard on /t/, and is
  literally a synonym for sharpen. Nobody in fitness owns it.
- **Why it might not.** Homophone with "wet" — bad-baggage risk for a sport
  brand. Tense test fails: "I whetted the plan" is awkward. Most readers only
  know the word from "whet the appetite" — one line of explanation required.
- **CTA test.** "Whet the plan tonight." Reads literary rather than
  imperative.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** Loses. Sharpen is unambiguous; Whet asks the
  user to hear a synonym they don't quite own.
- **Availability read.** whet.app / whet.fit plausible open.

### 5. Edge

- **Origin.** Old English *ecg* — the sharpened side of a blade; also the
  competitive advantage.
- **Why it fits.** Double meaning — the physical edge of the tool AND the
  edge the athlete earns. "Get your edge" and "sharpen your edge" are both
  native English.
- **Why it might not.** Microsoft Edge, Ford Edge, F45 Edge, U2's guitarist,
  ~400 fitness brands. SEO is unwinnable. Verb "edge" also carries an NSFW
  slang meaning that a 25-year-old audience will notice.
- **CTA test.** "Edge the plan tonight." Reads off due to slang.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** Loses. Slang risk is fatal for a young-adult
  fitness brand.
- **Availability read.** Dead.

### 6. Temper

- **Origin.** Latin *temperare* — to reheat a blade after quenching so it's
  both hard and tough. The metallurgical balance step.
- **Why it fits.** The most thematically precise metaphor for what an
  adaptive engine does — it balances push and hold, load and recover,
  hardness and toughness. Two syllables, but keeps the T monogram from Terav.
- **Why it might not.** "Temper" also means mood ("lose your temper"). The
  competing meaning will show up in copy occasionally. Same syllable count as
  Sharpen — no brevity gain.
- **CTA test.** "Temper the plan tonight." Reads clean.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Beats Sharpen on metaphor precision** (temper
  = balance, not just sharpen) and inherits the T monogram Terav already owns.
  Loses on transparency — an average reader doesn't know the metallurgy
  reading.
- **Availability read.** temper.works is a staffing app (Netherlands).
  temper.fit / temper.app plausible.

### 7. Calibrate

- **Origin.** Latin *qalib* via French — to align a measuring instrument
  against a standard.
- **Why it fits.** The single most precise word for what the app does — the
  engine calibrates load against readiness data. Also gym-adjacent: coaches
  say "let's calibrate your setup."
- **Why it might not.** Three syllables. Corporate register (lab equipment,
  meeting-jargon). Too clinical for a lifter to want on a hoodie.
- **CTA test.** "Calibrate the plan tonight." Reads accurate but stiff. On a
  button it feels like an admin action.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** Loses. Longer than Sharpen without winning any
  axis; also cold.
- **Availability read.** calibrate.com is a large weight-loss brand — direct
  fitness collision. Ruled out.

### 8. Lock (as in "lock in")

- **Origin.** English idiom "lock in" — CrossFit, esports, focus culture. To
  lock in is to bring your full attention to the task.
- **Why it fits.** "Lock in" is one of the two or three most-used athletic
  imperatives of the last five years — every CrossFit coach in North America
  says it daily. Monosyllable, hard /k/ plosive close.
- **Why it might not.** Same problem as Dial — the idiom is "lock in," two
  words. "Lock" alone reads mechanical / password-manager. Also very common:
  Lock, LockIn, and dozens of security apps use it.
- **CTA test.** "Lock the plan tonight." Reads slightly off — the phrase is
  "lock in tonight," not "lock the plan."
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** Loses. Insider status is very high but the
  standalone word doesn't carry the "refine" semantic — it means "commit to,"
  not "improve." Wrong verb.
- **Availability read.** Dead in obvious slots.

### 9. Prime

- **Origin.** English — peak state, or to prepare a surface / pump / person
  for work.
- **Why it fits.** "Prime the pump," "prime the CNS," "prime your setup" —
  native strength-and-conditioning language. Monosyllable, /pr/ cluster
  opens like "press" and "prep."
- **Why it might not.** Amazon Prime. Prime Hydration (Logan Paul). Prime is
  the most-owned single-syllable word in commerce. SEO catastrophic.
- **CTA test.** "Prime the plan tonight." Reads clean but reads like
  Amazon-adjacent supplements.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** Loses on availability. On tone alone it beats
  Sharpen (more athletic-native) but the brand-collision math is fatal.
- **Availability read.** Dead everywhere.

### 10. Carve

- **Origin.** Old English *ceorfan* — to cut with deliberate control, to
  shape by removal. Also snowboarding/skiing turn technique.
- **Why it fits.** Monosyllable, /k/ plosive open, /v/ soft close.
  Metaphorically it's the sculptor's verb: you don't add mass, you remove
  everything that isn't the sculpture. Which is exactly what the app does to a
  cookie-cutter program — carves away the sessions that aren't right for you.
- **Why it might not.** "Carve" is heavily associated with the physique/
  bodybuilding world ("carve your abs") — leans exactly into the gym-bro
  register the brief bans. Also skiing/surfing brand overlap.
- **CTA test.** "Carve the plan tonight." Reads native but the abs reading is
  hard to escape.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** Loses. Sharpen is clean; Carve carries physique
  culture baggage.
- **Availability read.** carve.com blocked (surf apparel). carve.fit likely
  gym-adjacent collision.

### 11. Refine

- **Origin.** Latin *refinare* — to purify metal, to remove impurities.
- **Why it fits.** Direct synonym for what the engine does. Softer than
  Sharpen; more chemistry than blade. Warm, non-tech register.
- **Why it might not.** Two syllables, no brevity gain. Iamb ("re-FINE"),
  which is corporate rhythm, not athletic rhythm. Also very generic — every
  luxury brand uses "refined" as a filler adjective.
- **CTA test.** "Refine the plan tonight." Reads clean but reads corporate.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** Loses. Same syllable count, worse rhythm,
  softer semantic.
- **Availability read.** refine.com is a data-cleaning tool. refine.fit
  plausible.

### 12. Aim

- **Origin.** Old French *esmer* — to estimate, to direct at a target.
- **Why it fits.** Monosyllable, front-vowel /eɪ/, clean /m/ close. Native
  athletic verb — you aim a lift, aim a program, aim your training. The
  "personalisation on your weakness" thesis is exactly "aim the work at the
  gap."
- **Why it might not.** AOL Instant Messenger owned the acronym for a
  generation (dead now but still a search hit). Also several fitness brands
  use "Aim" as a modifier. Verb is generic — anyone can aim anything.
- **CTA test.** "Aim the plan tonight." Reads decent — like a coach cue.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** Loses on distinctiveness. Aim is a strong
  verb but doesn't say "improve"; it says "point at." Slightly off the
  Sharpen semantic.
- **Availability read.** aim.com dead. aim.fit likely open.

### 13. Notch

- **Origin.** Old French *oche* — a small cut, a groove; also
  "notching up a PR."
- **Why it fits.** Monosyllable, /n/ open, /tʃ/ close — percussive. Doubles
  as progression verb (notch a PR) and mark verb (notch a session). Native
  lifter vocabulary.
- **Why it might not.** iPhone notch, Markus "Notch" Persson (Minecraft),
  Notch (Berlin motion-capture company). Also drifts from "sharpen" toward
  "count."
- **CTA test.** "Notch the plan tonight." Reads like a checkoff — actually
  good for a training log.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** Draws. Wins on brevity and insider status;
  loses on semantic precision — notching is *marking*, not *refining*.
- **Availability read.** notch.com blocked; notch.fit / notch.app plausible.

### 14. Forge

- **Origin.** Old French *forgier* — the fire where metal is shaped; also
  the verb "to form under heat and pressure."
- **Why it fits.** Monosyllable, /f/ open, /dʒ/ affricate close. Native gym
  poster language ("forged in the box"). Nordic-legible, Latin-legible.
- **Why it might not.** CrossFit Forge is a common gym name. Forge magazine.
  Forge Hero. Salesforce Forge. GitHub Forge patterns. Distinctiveness low
  and semantically it's the wrong verb — Forge is *make from nothing*, not
  *refine what exists*.
- **CTA test.** "Forge the plan tonight." Reads native but cliché.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** Loses. Sharpen is refine; Forge is create.
  Wrong metaphor for an adaptive engine that starts from a plan and improves
  it.
- **Availability read.** Dead in obvious slots.

### 15. Prime (variant: Peak)

- **Origin.** English — the highest point; peak week, peaking phase.
- **Why it fits.** Native to strength sports — peaking is a formal
  periodisation phase. Monosyllable, /p/ open, /k/ close — clean plosive
  bracket.
- **Why it might not.** Peak Performance (apparel), Peak Design (camera bags),
  Peaked (SaaS), Peak Games. Also semantically limits the app to peaking
  phase — the app also does base and rehab.
- **CTA test.** "Peak the plan tonight." Reads odd — you peak *for* something,
  not *the plan*.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** Loses. Wrong verb (peak is a state, not an
  action on the plan).
- **Availability read.** Blocked in obvious slots.

### 16. Fettle

- **Origin.** Northern English / Scots — to trim, to finish, to put right. A
  metal-caster's verb: to fettle a casting is to clean off the rough bits
  after it comes out of the mould. Also the noun "in fine fettle" = in good
  condition.
- **Why it fits.** Absurdly precise metaphor — after the rough cast (the
  template program) the app fettles it into the finished piece (your program).
  Nobody owns it. Two syllables but a trochee, and the "in fine fettle" idiom
  is native British English for peak condition.
- **Why it might not.** Deeply regional. Most US readers have never heard the
  word. Requires an explainer, which the brief rules against.
- **CTA test.** "Fettle the plan tonight." Reads like a private joke.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** Loses on transparency by a mile. Interesting
  as a wildcard, dead on delivery for an international audience.
- **Availability read.** fettle.com is a UK bike-repair app — direct
  fitness-adjacent collision. Ruled out.

### 17. Terav + English tagline (keep the mark, add the verb)

- **Origin.** Existing Estonian mark. "Sharp." Tagline solves the "explain the
  Estonian" problem: **"Terav — sharpen the plan."**
- **Why it fits.** Zero rebrand cost. Keeps the T monogram, keeps the
  existing icon work, keeps the "one physical verb" DNA. Every CTA and piece
  of copy already uses "sharpen" — the tagline simply makes that visible.
  Estonian roots are a story asset ("built by an Estonian coach, sharpened
  every session") not a liability, if the tagline carries the meaning in
  English.
- **Why it might not.** International users still have to learn the mark.
  "Terav" is not immediately parseable in English — the T sounds ambiguously
  between "TAIR-av" and "TER-uv." Two words to say instead of one for the
  brand.
- **CTA test.** The CTA doesn't change: "Sharpen the plan tonight." The
  brand is Terav; the verb is Sharpen. This is how Nike does it — the mark
  is a proper noun, the verb ("Just do it") is separate.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Beats Sharpen on brand-distinctiveness**
  (Sharpen as a mark reads as a feature; Terav reads as a name) but loses on
  first-touch legibility. If the audience is willing to learn one Estonian
  word — and CrossFitters learn Estonian names constantly (Rich Froning is
  from Tennessee but athletes learn Vinnicombe, Panchik, Ohlsen, Toomey,
  Duchesne, Sats) — this wins.
- **Availability read.** Terav.com currently the brand. No rebrand
  necessary.

### 18. Sharpen (baseline)

- **Origin.** Direct English of Terav. Already the internal verb.
- **Why it fits.** Transparency win — the brand IS the promise, no explainer.
  Verb-forward. Every piece of product copy already uses it. CTA writes
  itself. Reads clean in every market where English works.
- **Why it might not.** Two syllables and three consonant clusters — harder
  to shout than one-syllable options. "Sharpen" as a brand mark reads more
  like a feature name than a brand ("the sharpen tool"). Sharpen (contact-
  center software) owns the .com.
- **CTA test.** "Sharpen the plan tonight." The cleanest CTA in this document.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** N/A — this is the benchmark.
- **Availability read.** sharpen.com blocked; sharpen.app / sharpen.fit
  plausible; **very likely need a workaround domain like getsharpen.com or
  sharpn.co.**

## Sharpen-adjacent phrases (2–3 word candidates)

Testing short phrases as brand names in commerce. The bar: does it work on
the app store, on a domain, on a hoodie, and as an @-handle?

- **Sharp Edge.** Reads generic — a thousand knives, tools, and consulting
  firms. Domain dead. Fails the app-name test.
- **Fine Point.** Nice tactile phrase but reads stationery (pens). Fails the
  athletic-context test.
- **Fine Edge.** Slightly better than Sharp Edge — has a whisky/blade
  register. Still generic; sharpedge.com etc all taken.
- **Dial In.** The most-used precision idiom in the target audience's
  vocabulary. Reads like a coach cue. **Problem: dialin.com and Dialed In
  brand collision are both real.** dialin.app plausible. If domain works,
  this is a viable app name — but the mark is contested.
- **Lock In.** Same category as Dial In — pure athletic imperative,
  universally understood. **Problem: password/security tech dominates
  "LockIn" searches; also LockIn is a Netflix Korean drama.** Fails
  distinctiveness.
- **Zero In.** Precision idiom: "zero in on your weakness." Great fit for the
  personalisation thesis. **Problem: Zero is a massive brand family (Zero
  Motorcycles, Zero the sugar drink, Zero Trust) and ZeroIn is an existing
  SaaS.** Also "zero" reads negative in a fitness context (zero effort, zero
  progress).
- **Sharpen Up.** Coach's imperative — reads native on the box wall. Two
  words, six syllables total (SHAR-pen-UP). **Verdict: better as a tagline
  ("Sharpen up.") than a brand mark. Sharpenup.com almost certainly free but
  the phrase is too phrase-y to survive as a standalone app name.**
- **Peak Prep.** Reads like a periodisation phase name, not a brand. Also
  Peak Performance apparel collision. Fails.
- **Prime Cycle.** Reads like a supplement or a spin class. Fails.
- **Peak Load.** Sports-science term of art (peak-load training). Would
  confuse users into thinking the app is only for peaking phases. Fails as
  a general brand.
- **The Grind.** Round 1 rightly rejected. The product's whole pitch is the
  opposite of grind culture. Fails on brand alignment.
- **The Whet.** Interesting — noun-form of the verb, definite article
  positions the app as *the sharpening tool*. thewhet.com plausible. But
  same problem as Whet alone: homophone with "wet," and readers stumble on
  meaning.
- **One Notch.** Reads as a progression tagline ("one notch stronger every
  week"). Not viable as a brand name — reads phrase-y, and dilutes the
  progression semantic across two words.
- **One Cut.** Barber/sushi/butcher imagery competes. Fails category.
- **One Rep.** Very insider ("one rep max" = 1RM). Great tagline energy, but
  as a brand it locks the app to strength (excludes aerobic/skill/rehab
  content). Fails scope.

**Verdict on phrases:** none survive as an app-store brand name. Two-word
brands are hard to defend in commerce — you can't trademark generic phrases
easily, the domains are contested, and users abbreviate them anyway. The
strongest phrases (Dial In, Sharpen Up) are **better used as taglines under
a one-word brand** than as brands themselves.

## Invented / hybrid Sharpen-family names

The founder's discomfort with tech-VC syllable-smashups is real, but a few
constructions are worth testing.

- **Sharpn.** Dropped-vowel Sharpen. Reads modern (Grindr, Tumblr, Flickr
  lineage) but exactly the "startup-tech" tell the audience is suspicious of.
  Also pronunciation-ambiguous — is it "SHARP-en" or "SHARP-in"? Fails
  the brief's tone test.
- **Sharpr.** Same problem, worse readability. Fails.
- **Sharpen.io.** Reads as the .io-fied version of the feature. Domain
  workaround, not a brand solution. **Would use sharpen.app or getsharpen.com
  before this.**
- **Whetco.** Reads as a small-batch brand (like a knife-maker or coffee
  roaster). Not a fitness app. Fails category, though it has a certain
  independent charm.
- **Honefit.** "Hone" + "fit" — the classic fitness suffix. Reads generic and
  loses everything that made Hone distinctive. Fails.
- **Sharp.** Truncated Sharpen. Sharp Electronics owns the mark; Sharp (the
  common English word) is uncontrollable in SEO. Also "sharp" isn't quite a
  verb ("I sharped today" fails the tense test — see Round 1 Agent 3).
- **Whettr.** Dropped-vowel Whet. Same problems as Sharpn plus the "wet"
  homophone. Fails.
- **Terav.io / Terav.app.** Domain workaround for keeping the current brand.
  Not a rename.

**Verdict on invented forms:** none beat Sharpen. The dropped-vowel forms
carry exactly the startup-tech aftertaste the brief bans, and the suffix
forms (Honefit, Whetco) throw away the distinctiveness of the root. If a
short domain is the actual blocker, **the answer is a prefix on the real
word (getsharpen.com, usesharpen.com) or a .app/.fit TLD, not a mutilated
spelling.**

## Shortlist — top 3

### 1. Hone

The strongest one-syllable substitute for Sharpen. Same semantic (refine a
blade), tighter cadence (one syllable, not two), and it's already in the
target audience's vocabulary — no lifter has to be taught what "hone your
technique" means. The CTA barely changes: "Sharpen the plan tonight"
becomes "Hone tonight's session" and reads cleaner because it's shorter.
The trademark risk (Hone Health, Hone HQ) is real but categorically distant
enough to survive with a chiselled-stone monogram and a fitness-Class-9
registration. Domain math is workable: hone.fit, hone.app, or usehone.com.

### 2. Temper

The most thematically precise name in the Sharpen family. Tempering is the
metallurgical balance step — the reheat after the quench that keeps a blade
hard *and* tough. That's a better metaphor for an adaptive engine than
"sharpen" is: the engine doesn't just make things sharper, it balances push
and hold every day. Two syllables, so no brevity gain over Sharpen, but the
monogram inherits directly from Terav (T stays T) and the box-wall test wins
("I'm on Temper this cycle" reads native). The "mood" reading of the word is
a manageable risk; in product copy, the training reading dominates.

### 3. Terav — sharpen the plan (keep the mark, add the tagline)

The strongest zero-rebrand option. The rename brief exists because "Terav"
is unfamiliar in English, but the fix might not be renaming — it might be
attaching the English verb as a permanent tagline. "Terav — sharpen the
plan" reads as a brand plus a promise. Nike does exactly this (Nike is a
proper noun; "Just do it" is the verb). The founder keeps the T monogram,
keeps the chisel-stroke icon work, keeps the Estonian story (which is an
asset for a CrossFit audience that already learns Estonian, Icelandic,
Australian, Norwegian athlete names). The tagline carries the meaning
where the mark can't.

Cut with regret: **Notch** (draws with Sharpen on brevity but loses on
semantic precision — the app refines, it doesn't mark). **Dial (in)** (best
insider vocabulary but the standalone word is bland; the idiom needs two
words). Everything else fails at least one gate.

## If I were writing the cheque

**Terav — sharpen the plan.** Keep the mark. Attach the English verb as the
permanent tagline everywhere the brand appears. The founder's whole reason
for liking Sharpen — that every CTA already uses it, that it's transparent,
that it's verb-forward — is preserved as a tagline; the brand-distinctiveness
that a one-word English brand can't defend is preserved by keeping Terav.
Nobody else in fitness owns "Terav." Nobody in fitness has a story like "an
Estonian word for sharp." That's an asset, not a liability, in a market
where the audience is used to learning foreign athlete names.

If the founder wants a genuine rename, **Hone** is the answer — closest
one-to-one substitute for Sharpen, shorter, already in the lifter's mouth,
survives every CTA rewrite with a search-and-replace. Trademark risk is
manageable with a chisel-stroke monogram and category discipline.

If the founder wants a rename that says something Sharpen can't, **Temper**
is the answer — the metallurgical balance metaphor matches an adaptive
