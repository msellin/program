# Naming — Round 3, Agent 1: Estonian-First

Angle: **build the brand from an Estonian root that also travels in English.**
The founder is Estonian. First 50 users are local. Estonian marketing copy
must feel native — no calque, no translated-English tell. But the app also
launches in English, so the mark must not read alien on an American
CrossFit floor.

The problem Round 2 exposed: **Sharpen works in English, Terav does not
verb in Estonian.** "Terita oma plaani" reads like a translated English
metaphor to an Estonian ear. The natural Estonian verb is **lihvida**
(to polish/refine) or **sättida** (to adjust). Neither has anything to
do with the current brand mark.

The bet this agent makes: the honest fix is not to translate one market
into the other, but to pick a name that either (a) sits inside Estonian
natively and stands as a foreign-confident brand mark in English (Ikea,
Nokia, Skype pattern) or (b) rides an Estonian tagline that carries the
verb work in Estonian without breaking the English-first CTA.

Every candidate is stress-tested against Sharpen on both the English CTA
test and the Estonian CTA test. A candidate has to fix the Estonian-verb
problem OR beat Sharpen in English — ideally both — to be worth the
switching cost.

---

## Estonian root inventory (pre-work)

Before candidates, the raw material. Estonian roots that could name a
training app, with the natural verb form and what it actually means on
a Tallinn gym floor.

| Root | Verb | Meaning | Register |
|------|------|---------|----------|
| **lihv** | lihvida / lihvi | grind, polish, refine (skill or blade) | native + universal |
| **tera** | teritada / terita | sharpen (blade, pencil — physical only) | native but physical |
| **serv** | servida (rare) | edge; edging is technical (metal, ski) | native, narrow |
| **sätt** | sättida / sätti | adjust, arrange, put in place | native, casual |
| **piik** | piigata (rare) | peak (loanword drift) | half-loan |
| **vorm** | vormida / vormi ("olen vormis" = "I'm in shape") | form, physical condition | native + athletic |
| **vaim** | (no direct verb; "vaimuga" = "with spirit") | spirit | native, poetic |
| **trots** | trotsida / trotsi | defiance, defy | native, athletic |
| **tuum** | (no verb) | core, kernel | native, technical |
| **tulem** | (no verb — "tulemus" = result) | result, outcome | native, formal |
| **hoog** | hoogsalt / hoogu andma ("gather momentum") | momentum, drive | native, athletic |
| **kraad** | (no verb) | degree (temperature, precision) | native, measurement |
| **siht** | sihtida / sihti | target, aim | native, precise |

The strongest verb-carrying roots are **lihv**, **sätt**, **siht**, and
**vorm**. The strongest brand-mark roots (say-once, forget-nothing) are
**tera**, **serv**, **tuum**, and **trots**. The candidates that follow
are drawn from both columns, plus creative combinations and one
Terav-with-Estonian-tagline test.

## Candidates (14)

### 1. Lihv

- **Origin.** Estonian noun *lihv* — grind, polish, chamfer, the finish
  step. The verb *lihvida* / imperative *lihvi* is the exact word an
  Estonian coach uses to mean "polish your technique." Also non-athletic
  Estonian: *lihvitud* (polished, refined) is what you call a well-crafted
  presentation, a good pianist, a diamond. This is the highest-value
  Estonian noun in the whole inventory for a training brand.
- **Why it fits.** Four letters, one syllable, /lɪhv/. Plosive /l/ open,
  fricative /hv/ close — the /hv/ cluster is exotic in English but
  Estonians read it clean. Monogram L is workable and matches a chisel-
  stroke or fine-blade mark. The verb IS the promise: the app lihvs your
  plan. Estonian users see the brand name in every native CTA
  ("Lihvi plaani täna õhtul" = "Lihv the plan tonight") without any
  translation tax.
- **English CTA test.** "Lihv the plan tonight." Reads foreign but
  parseable — same tier as "Whoop your session," "Notion your notes,"
  "Nokia your call." English users don't need to know it means "polish";
  they learn the brand-as-verb the same way they learned "google it."
- **Estonian CTA test.** "Lihvi oma plaani." Native. Zero translation
  tax. This is how an Estonian coach would say it if the brand didn't
  exist. **The brand is the verb the language already uses.**
- **Cross-market brand test.** American CrossFitter says "I use Lihv" —
  slight stumble on first read (/lɪv/ or /lɪhv/?) but no worse than
  "I use Whoop" or "I use Zwift." Tallinn CrossFitter says "Ma kasutan
  Lihvi" — zero cringe, reads like a normal Estonian brand (Bolt, Wolt,
  Skype lineage).
- **Trademark / domain read.** Lihv is not a claimed brand in fitness.
  lihv.com almost certainly free or parked; lihv.app / lihv.fit /
  uselihv.com plausible. Zero claimed collisions in EU or US fitness
  registers on quick check. Estonian-language origin makes the trademark
  fight cleaner in EU class 9/41.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Beats Sharpen on the exact problem
  Round 3 was called to solve.** Sharpen breaks in Estonian; Lihv works
  in both markets. The English CTA is one learning step (audience learns
  a foreign brand-as-verb) which every consumer app in the last 20 years
  has proven the audience does. Loses to Sharpen only on English
  first-touch parseability — but the founder was never going to win that
  race with a two-syllable feature-name English word anyway.

### 2. Tera

- **Origin.** Estonian noun *tera* — blade, kernel, grain of wheat. Root
  of *terav* (sharp) and *teritada* (to sharpen). Four letters, two
  syllables (TE-ra). Every Estonian knows the word.
- **Why it fits.** Softer, warmer version of Terav — the object (blade)
  instead of the adjective (sharp). Reads friendly in both languages.
  In English, "Tera" reads like Terra (earth) or a name (Teresa
  short-form) — familiar-adjacent. Monogram T carries over cleanly from
  Terav — zero design cost. The bronze chisel-stroke works unchanged.
- **English CTA test.** "Tera the plan tonight." Fails as verb — same
  problem as Terav. Tera is a noun; you can't Tera something.
- **Estonian CTA test.** "Tera oma plaani." Fails identically — tera is
  a noun in Estonian too. Doesn't verb in either language.
- **Cross-market brand test.** American CrossFitter says "I use Tera" —
  reads clean, warm, feminine-adjacent (which may be a bug or a feature
  for a rehab-heavy brand). Tallinn CrossFitter says "Ma kasutan Terat"
  — reads clean, though slightly odd because *tera* is such a common
  common-noun (the blade of your knife) that using it as a brand feels
  like naming a brand "Blade."
- **Trademark / domain read.** Tera is heavily used — Tera compute
  (terabyte prefix), Terra (Luna crypto brand collapse), Terra Kaffe,
  many small brands. Domain fight tough. tera.fit maybe open.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Loses to Sharpen.** Doesn't fix the
  verb problem in either language — same failure as Terav, just with
  softer edges. If Terav doesn't verb, Tera doesn't either.

### 3. Serv

- **Origin.** Estonian noun *serv* — edge (of a blade, of a shape, of a
  road). Verb form *servima* exists but is technical (metalwork ski
  edging). Four letters, one syllable.
- **Why it fits.** Sharp semantic overlap with Terav — the edge IS the
  sharp part. Monogram S clean. Estonian reads native, though the verb
  is niche.
- **English CTA test.** "Serv the plan tonight." Fails — English readers
  will read it as "Serve" (typo) or think of Serbian short-form. Also
  collides with tennis "serve." Ambiguous parse kills it.
- **Estonian CTA test.** "Serv oma plaani." Half-works — Estonians know
  the noun but the verb form (*servi* imperative) reads odd for skill
  refinement; it's a metalworker's or skier's word. A coach wouldn't
  say "servi oma tehnikat."
- **Cross-market brand test.** American user says "Serv" and reads
  "server" adjacent (tech register). Estonian user says "Serv" and reads
  "edge" but wonders why the app is called that.
- **Trademark / domain read.** serv.com is a tech-services brand; serv.fit
  maybe open. Ambiguity with "serve" makes SEO catastrophic.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Loses to Sharpen.** Wrong verb in
  Estonian (metalworker's, not coach's), homograph collision in English.
  Interesting on paper, fails on delivery.

### 4. Sätt

- **Origin.** Estonian noun *sätt* — setting, arrangement. Verb *sättima*
  / imperative *sätti* means "adjust, put in place." Universal casual
  Estonian: "sätti see paika" = "set that in place," "sätti end mugavalt"
  = "settle in comfortably." Four letters, one syllable, /sæt/ish.
- **Why it fits.** The verb is exactly what the adaptive engine does —
  it sätts (adjusts, arranges) the plan around your data. Native, casual,
  warm register. Monogram S is workable. The umlaut /ä/ is a visual
  distinguisher and reads as European-quirky in English (like Häagen-Dazs
  or Motörhead) — a feature, not a bug.
- **English CTA test.** "Sätt the plan tonight." Reads foreign but not
  hostile; English users pronounce it "sat" or "set" and both work.
  Closer to English "set" than any other Estonian root — accidental
  cognate helps parse.
- **Estonian CTA test.** "Sätti oma plaani." Native. Zero translation
  tax. This is exactly how an Estonian coach or physio would casually
  say "adjust your plan."
- **Cross-market brand test.** American CrossFitter says "I use Sätt" —
  the umlaut is a mild speedbump but readers will settle on /sæt/ or
  /sɛt/ within two exposures. Tallinn CrossFitter says "Ma kasutan Sätti"
  — reads native, casual, warm.
- **Trademark / domain read.** Sätt is not a claimed brand in fitness.
  ASCII fallback "satt.app" or "usesatt.com" plausible. The umlaut is a
  brand-mark asset (like Skype's "kype" was distinctive) but a domain
  liability — need ASCII fallback URL.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Beats Sharpen on the Estonian side and
  ties in English.** "Sätti oma plaani" reads native Estonian; "Sharpen
  the plan" reads native English; "Sätt the plan" reads as
  brand-as-verb (foreign but confident) in English. The umlaut is either
  the whole story (distinctive) or a friction cost (domain, keyboard).

### 5. Vorm

- **Origin.** Estonian noun *vorm* — form, shape, physical condition.
  "Olen heas vormis" = "I'm in good shape / in good form." Loanword-
  cognate with English "form," German "Form," etc. Four letters, one
  syllable.
- **Why it fits.** Universal athletic vocabulary in Estonian — everyone
  uses it. The app is literally about form/shape. Loanword-adjacent, so
  English users parse it as "form" almost automatically.
- **English CTA test.** "Vorm the plan tonight." Fails — English readers
  read it as "Form" and wonder why it's misspelled. Doesn't work as verb
  in English.
- **Estonian CTA test.** "Vormi oma plaani." Half-works — *vormima* means
  "to shape/mold" but for a plan or skill it reads slightly industrial
  (mold a plan). Not the natural coach verb.
- **Cross-market brand test.** American user reads "Vorm" as a typo of
  "Form" or a German loanword. Estonian user reads it as "form" but the
  verb usage is off-register.
- **Trademark / domain read.** vorm.com is a Dutch construction firm;
  vorm.fit maybe open. Loanword overlap with German/Dutch complicates EU
  trademark.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Loses to Sharpen.** Verb-fail in English,
  off-register verb in Estonian. The cognate is close enough to English
  that users perceive it as a misspelling rather than a foreign brand —
  the worst-of-both outcome.

### 6. Trots

- **Origin.** Estonian noun *trots* — defiance, spite, "against the
  odds." Verb *trotsida* / imperative *trotsi* = "to defy, to resist."
  Athletic register: "trotsi valu" = "defy the pain." Five letters, one
  syllable.
- **Why it fits.** Emotionally athletic — trots is the mindset that gets
  a lifter under a heavy bar on a bad day. Estonian native, confident,
  slightly punk. Reads as brand-attitude, not brand-feature.
- **English CTA test.** "Trots the plan tonight." Fails — English
  readers read "trot" (light run) and think this is a running app for
  horses. Category confusion fatal.
- **Estonian CTA test.** "Trotsi oma plaani." Reads dramatic — you defy
  your plan? Wrong semantic. Trots is a mindset against adversity, not
  an act of refinement. Doesn't fit the confirm-first, adaptive-engine
  philosophy at all.
- **Cross-market brand test.** American user reads "trotting." Estonian
  user reads "defiance" but wonders why a training app is asking them
  to defy their plan.
- **Trademark / domain read.** Small Nordic brands use "trots" (Swedish
  cognate). Domain fight moderate.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Loses to Sharpen.** Wrong semantic in
  both languages, English homograph collision. Attitude is right but the
  verb-plan interaction is wrong.

### 7. Tuum

- **Origin.** Estonian noun *tuum* — core, kernel, nucleus. Four letters,
  one syllable, /tuːm/. Very Estonian ("tuumaenergia" = nuclear energy;
  "tuumik" = core group). No native verb form.
- **Why it fits.** Semantic overlap with "core training" is real — the
  app IS about your core (weakness, focus). Short, distinctive, easy on
  the mouth in both languages.
- **English CTA test.** "Tuum the plan tonight." Fails as verb — no verb
  form exists in either language. Also English readers read "toom" and
  puzzle.
- **Estonian CTA test.** "Tuum oma plaani." Fails — noun, no verb form.
- **Cross-market brand test.** American user says "Tuum" and it reads
  clean-foreign, warm on the mouth. Tallinn user says "Tuum" and reads
  it as "core" (Latin loan register). Both parse but neither verbs.
- **Trademark / domain read.** tuum.com is a UK fintech (core banking
  platform). Direct semantic collision (they literally use "tuum = core"
  as the marketing story). Trademark fight lost before it starts.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Loses to Sharpen.** No verb form, and
  the .com is owned by a fintech using the exact same "tuum = core"
  positioning. Blocked at the trademark step.

### 8. Siht

- **Origin.** Estonian noun *siht* — target, aim. Verb *sihtida* /
  imperative *sihti* = "to aim, to target." "Sihikindel" = "purposeful."
  Four letters, one syllable.
- **Why it fits.** The personalisation-on-your-weakness thesis IS a
  siht — the app aims the training at the gap. Native Estonian verb
  (a coach or physio would say "sihti oma nõrkust" = "target your
  weakness"). Precise semantic.
- **English CTA test.** "Siht the plan tonight." Fails — English readers
  read "sight" (typo) or worse, an obscene homophone. Fatal.
- **Estonian CTA test.** "Sihti oma plaani." Reads slightly formal but
  clean — coaches say "sihi" more than "sihti." Half-native.
- **Cross-market brand test.** American user reads "Siht" and either
  laughs or thinks it's a typo. Fatal English-market read.
- **Trademark / domain read.** Small use; siht.com maybe open. But the
  English-market slur homophone means this dies before trademark matters.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Loses to Sharpen.** English homophone
  makes the word unusable in international market. This is the "Fartein"
  problem — a legitimate Nordic word that English audiences can't take
  seriously.

### 9. Hoog

- **Origin.** Estonian noun *hoog* — momentum, drive, sweep. "Hoogu
  andma" = "to give momentum, to gather steam." Athletic native ("hoogne
  jooks" = a sweeping/vigorous run). Four letters, one syllable, /hoːɡ/.
- **Why it fits.** Momentum is exactly the progression story — session
  by session, block by block, hoog builds. Estonian native, confident,
  slightly poetic. Warm on the mouth.
- **English CTA test.** "Hoog the plan tonight." Fails — English reads
  "hoog" as gibberish or "huge" mispronounced. No verb parse.
- **Estonian CTA test.** "Anna oma plaanile hoogu" (give your plan
  momentum) is the natural phrase — but the brand name Hoog doesn't
  itself verb naturally. Estonians would use *hoogustada* (rare) or the
  give-momentum idiom, but wouldn't say "hoogi oma plaani."
- **Cross-market brand test.** American user reads "Hoog" and struggles.
  Tallinn user reads "Hoog" as a poetic noun but wonders why the brand
  isn't verbing.
- **Trademark / domain read.** hoog.com is Dutch for "high" — brand
  collision with several Dutch companies. Domain fight moderate.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Loses to Sharpen.** Verb doesn't fire
  cleanly in either language despite the beautiful semantic.

### 10. Teralihv (compound: blade-polish)

- **Origin.** Compound of *tera* (blade) + *lihv* (polish). Not a
  standard Estonian word — an invented compound, but grammatically clean.
  Estonians build compounds this way constantly (jäätisemees = ice-cream
  man). Eight letters, three syllables.
- **Why it fits.** Explicitly bridges Terav's blade imagery and Lihv's
  refine-verb. The brand-name = the whole story in one word: sharpen the
  blade, polish the edge. Distinctive in both markets.
- **English CTA test.** "Teralihv the plan tonight." Fails — three
  syllables, foreign, unmemorable. Too many syllables to shout.
- **Estonian CTA test.** "Teralihvi oma plaani." Grammatical but reads
  invented; nobody says "teralihvi" as a verb. Compounds don't casually
  verbify in Estonian.
- **Cross-market brand test.** Fails syllable count in both markets.
- **Trademark / domain read.** Fully open — nobody uses this compound —
  but that's because it's not really a word.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Loses to Sharpen.** Too long and too
  invented. The brand-etymology story is beautiful but nobody hears it
  on the box wall.

### 11. Servalihv (compound: edge-polish)

- **Origin.** *Serv* (edge) + *lihv* (polish). Same construction as
  Teralihv. Nine letters, three syllables.
- **Why it fits.** Same as above — precise semantic, bridge between
  blade-language and polish-verb.
- **English CTA test.** Fails identically. Three syllables, foreign,
  unmemorable.
- **Estonian CTA test.** Fails identically. Not a real Estonian word.
- **Cross-market brand test.** Same failure mode.
- **Trademark / domain read.** Open, meaningless.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Loses to Sharpen.** Same failure as
  Teralihv.

### 12. Sätt-Tera (compound: adjust-blade)

- **Origin.** Hybrid — the two best roots (Sätt = adjust, Tera = blade)
  joined. Grammatically nonstandard.
- **Why it fits.** Semantically it reads like "adjust the blade" which
  is a coherent training metaphor.
- **English CTA test.** Fails — hyphenated brands are dead in mobile
  app stores and CLI URLs. Nobody types "Sätt-Tera" without a mistake.
- **Estonian CTA test.** Fails — Estonians don't hyphenate compounds
  this way; they concatenate. And even concatenated (*sätttera*) it's
  not a word.
- **Cross-market brand test.** Fails in both markets.
- **Trademark / domain read.** N/A.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Loses to Sharpen.** Hyphenated brands
  don't survive.

### 13. Peak (English loanword Estonians already use)

- **Origin.** English "peak" — loanword some Estonians use in sports
  ("peaki nädalale" = peak week). Not native but familiar.
- **Why it fits.** Loanword-cognate approach — the word already works in
  both languages because both languages borrow it. One syllable, sharp
  plosive close.
- **English CTA test.** "Peak the plan tonight." Reads slightly off —
  you peak *for* an event, not the plan itself. Verb fails.
- **Estonian CTA test.** "Peaki oma plaani." Loanword-verb feels
  half-native; younger Estonians might, older wouldn't. Corporate register.
- **Cross-market brand test.** Both markets read it but the verb doesn't
  land cleanly.
- **Trademark / domain read.** Peak Performance apparel, Peak Design
  camera bags, Peaked SaaS — heavy prior art. Dead.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Loses to Sharpen.** Verb-fail plus
  catastrophic prior art.

### 14. Terav + Estonian tagline ("Terav — lihvi plaani")

- **Origin.** Keep existing mark. Attach a permanent Estonian imperative
  tagline that uses the natural Estonian refine-verb: "Terav — lihvi
  plaani" (Terav — polish/refine the plan). English tagline stays
  "Terav — sharpen the plan" (as Round 2 Agent 2 proposed).
- **Why it fits.** Zero rebrand cost. Keeps the T monogram, keeps the
  bronze chisel-stroke, keeps the existing icon and story. The brand mark
  stays Terav (proper noun, foreign-confident in both markets). The verb
  work is done by two different verbs in two different taglines — each
  one native to its own market. This is exactly the Nike / Just Do It
  pattern: the mark doesn't have to verb; the tagline does.
- **English CTA test.** "Sharpen the plan tonight." Native English CTA,
  unchanged from Round 2. The brand is Terav; the CTA uses "sharpen."
- **Estonian CTA test.** "Lihvi plaani täna õhtul." Native Estonian CTA,
  finally. The brand is Terav; the CTA uses "lihvi." This is the exact
  fix the brief called for — the Estonian copy uses the Estonian verb,
  not a forced "terita" calque.
- **Cross-market brand test.** American CrossFitter says "I use Terav"
  — foreign but confident, same tier as "I use Whoop" or "I use Zwift."
  Tallinn CrossFitter says "Ma kasutan Teravit" — reads native (Estonian
  brands often use Estonian words as marks — Bolt was Taxify; Wolt
  works; Skype was Estonian-built). No cringe.
- **Trademark / domain read.** Terav.com currently held by the brand.
  Zero migration cost.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Beats Sharpen decisively on the Estonian
  problem.** Sharpen fails Estonian copy; Terav-with-two-taglines
  succeeds in both markets. The English CTAs don't change (still
  "sharpen"). The Estonian CTAs become native ("lihvi"). The brand mark
  is distinctive in both markets (proper noun, no feature-name feel).
  The founder keeps every piece of existing design work. This is the
  cheapest, most honest fix the brief could produce — and it was hiding
  in plain sight in the round-2 report.

## Shortlist — top 3

### 1. Terav + Estonian tagline ("Terav — lihvi plaani")

Zero rebrand cost. Keeps the T monogram, the bronze chisel-stroke, the
existing icon, the existing story, the existing domain, the existing
product copy. The fix is not a new name — the fix is a second tagline,
in Estonian, that uses the natural Estonian verb (*lihvi* — polish,
refine) that a Tallinn coach would actually say. The English tagline
stays "Terav — sharpen the plan" so the English CTAs don't change.
Estonian users now see native Estonian copy ("Lihvi plaani täna õhtul"
= "Polish the plan tonight") that reads as native to a native speaker,
not as translated English calque. This is exactly the Nike pattern: the
mark is a proper noun in every market; the verb changes per market
because verbs always change per market. The founder's whole Round-2
hesitation was that Terav-as-mark forced a translated Estonian verb
(*terita*) that reads like a calque. The answer isn't to rename — it's
to let the mark be a mark and let each market's copy use each market's
verb. Cheapest fix that solves the exact problem the brief posed.

### 2. Lihv

The strongest full-rebrand option built from an Estonian root. *Lihv*
is the exact Estonian noun for the polish/refine step, and *lihvi* is
the natural imperative every Estonian coach uses for "polish your
technique" or "refine your setup." Four letters, one syllable, plosive
open, fricative close. Estonian users see the brand-as-verb ("Lihvi
plaani") with zero translation tax — this is the deepest native
Estonian fit any brand in this exercise achieved. English users learn
"Lihv" as a foreign-confident brand mark (Whoop, Zwift, Nokia lineage);
the verb-work is done by the brand itself (*Lihv the plan tonight*)
in the same way "google it" carries English. Trademark ground is clean.
Domain paths (lihv.app, lihv.fit, uselihv.com) plausible. The cost is
that the English audience has to learn a foreign brand mark from scratch
— but that cost was already priced into Terav, and Lihv verbs where
Terav doesn't. This is the honest full-rename option if the founder
decides the "brand IS the verb" story matters more than keeping the
existing chisel-stroke design work.

### 3. Sätt

The friendliest, most casual option. *Sätt* / *sättida* is the everyday
Estonian verb for "adjust, arrange, put in place" — exactly the
confirm-first adaptive engine's action ("the engine sätts, you accept").
Four letters, one syllable, and the umlaut /ä/ gives the mark visual
distinctiveness (Häagen-Dazs / Motörhead pattern) in English markets
without hurting parseability. English CTA works ("Sätt the plan
tonight") because the vowel-and-consonant shape is close enough to
"set" that English users converge to a pronunciation within two
exposures. Estonian CTA works natively ("Sätti oma plaani"). The
casual register is either an asset (approachable, non-tech) or a
mismatch (the brand is scientifically rigorous — 100+ studies — and
*sätt* is a homey word). Domain fight is easy; ASCII fallback needed
for URLs (satt.app / usesatt.com). Second-best full-rename option after
Lihv, with a warmer register.

Cut with regret: **Tera** (would have been beautiful — softer Terav —
but doesn't verb in either language, same problem the brief was written
to solve). **Hoog** (the momentum semantic is perfect for a progression
app, but the verb doesn't fire cleanly). **Tuum** (blocked by fintech
prior art with the same "tuum = core" story).

## If I were writing the cheque

**Terav — with permanent bilingual taglines. English CTA stays "sharpen
the plan." Estonian CTA becomes "lihvi plaani."**

The brief asked for a name that "either (a) works naturally in both
languages as a verb, OR (b) works as a brand mark in both languages
and doesn't force awkward translated CTAs — where 'kasutan Slacki'
works for Slack in Estonian because Slack doesn't try to also be the
verb." That's option (b), verbatim. Terav is a foreign-confident proper
noun in both markets — an Estonian word that reads as a name, not a
feature. The verb work is done by two different verbs in two different
taglines because that's how verbs work in bilingual product marketing:
they change per market. Sharpen in English. Lihvi in Estonian. The
mark stays Terav. The T monogram stays. The chisel-stroke stays. The
domain stays. The existing product copy stays in English. The Estonian
marketing copy — which the brief said "must feel native" — finally does,
because it uses the verb Estonians actually say (*lihvi*) instead of
the calqued *terita* that reads as translated English.

The founder's Round-2 hesitation was correct: Terav as a mark plus a
forced Estonian verb (*terita*) creates a broken Estonian CTA. But the
fix isn't to rebrand — it's to notice that the English brand ("Terav")
never had to be the verb in Estonian. Slack isn't the verb in Estonian
("Ma kasutan Slacki"); it's just a name. Terav can be just a name too,
carried by *lihvi* on the Estonian side and *sharpen* on the English
side. Two verbs, one mark, two markets. Zero rebrand cost. Every
existing piece of design work survives.

If the founder rejects that and wants a genuine full rename that fixes
both the verb problem AND the "foreign brand in English" problem in one
move, **Lihv** is the answer. Four letters, one syllable, the exact
Estonian verb for refine-your-skill, and a foreign-confident brand mark
in English that verbs (*"Lihv the plan tonight"*) the way Whoop and
Zwift verb. But this costs the existing T-monogram work and asks the
English audience to learn a new foreign word — a cost the founder is
already paying with Terav, so switching to Lihv trades one foreign brand
for another that at least verbs correctly in Estonian.

The one thing I would not do: pick **Sharpen** as the English brand mark
while keeping Terav in Estonian. That creates two separate brands in two
separate markets and doubles the trademark, design, and story cost while
solving the verb problem worse than Terav-with-Estonian-tagline does.
The Round-2 "Sharpen" recommendation solves the English problem at the
cost of the Estonian one — and the Estonian one is where the founder
lives and where the first 50 users are. **Terav with two verbs is the
honest fix. Lihv is the honest rename. Sharpen is the wrong tradeoff for
this specific founder in this specific market.**
