# Naming — Round 2, Agent 1: CrossFit Vocabulary Hunt

Angle: names that come from the vocabulary CrossFit / strength / rehab-adjacent
lifters actually SAY on the floor every week. The bet is that the audience
adopts a name faster if it's already in their mouth, and that the founder gets
a brand that speaks the register without translating.

The ghost in every head-to-head is **Sharpen** — the founder's preferred pick.
Sharpen wins on: transparency, English-of-Terav, verb-forward, every existing
piece of copy already uses it. A challenger has to beat those on at least one
dimension the audience cares about (mouth-feel, box-wall shoutability, native
gym vocabulary, semantic connection to "find and work your weakness").

---

## Vocabulary map (round-one work before candidates)

Before ranking candidates I walked the audience's actual vocabulary. This is
the raw pool the candidates below were drawn from — most of it is unusable
(too claimed, too generic, wrong tone) but the map matters for showing what
was rejected.

**Named workouts (the Girls and Heroes).**
Fran, Grace, Diane, Amanda, Angie, Cindy, Karen, Helen, Nancy, Barbara,
Isabel, Elizabeth, Jackie, Kelly, Linda, Murph, JT, DT, Chad, Roy, Josh,
Blake, Dan. *Cut wholesale.* Every one of these is either a person's name
(uncomfortable brand for an app) or CrossFit HQ IP with active enforcement.
Zero survive. Worth naming a *feature* after ("your Fran time" as a test
input) — never the app.

**Prescriptions and formats.**
WOD, Rx, scaled, AMRAP, EMOM, FGB, chipper, benchmark, metcon, hero, girl,
ladder, negative, tempo, buy-in, cash-out, round, cluster, complex, EMOTM,
death-by, tabata. *Rx* is the most tempting — it means "the prescribed
version" and the app literally writes prescriptions — but "Rx" is medical-
adjacent (Rx = prescription) which the brief bans, and the trademark is
CrossFit HQ's. **Chipper** made the shortlist below. **Ladder** made it.
**Tempo** ruled out (Tempo Fit owns it — $250M home-gym brand). **AMRAP** is
too format-specific.

**Movements and skills.**
Snatch, clean, jerk, deadlift, thruster, burpee, muscle-up, HSPU, C2B, T2B,
kip, butterfly, pistol, wall ball, box jump, DU, TGU, KB swing, front squat,
back squat, OHS. *Cut most.* Movement names as brand names read as niche
gimmick (an app called "Snatch" tells you nothing except "we teach that one
lift"). **Thruster** and **Kip** got included for stress-testing.

**Physiology talk.**
Engine, gas tank, capacity, threshold, VO2, aerobic, anaerobic, lactic,
taper, peak, spike, block, cycle, wheelhouse, ceiling, floor, base,
work capacity, GPP. This is the *richest* vein. Lifters read Chris Hinshaw
and Attia and use this language daily. **Engine**, **Capacity**, **Threshold**,
**Base**, **Wheelhouse**, **Ceiling** all considered. Two made the shortlist.

**Coaching cues / imperatives (verbs on the floor).**
"Dial it in," "lock in," "sharpen up," "tighten up," "prime the pump,"
"level up," "clean it up," "trim it," "own it," "build," "stack," "load,"
"unload," "hit the number," "make weight," "brace," "set," "reset," "cue,"
"pace," "hold your position," "own the position," "own the tempo." Verb
family is where a brand name lives — this is exactly the register the CTA
test rewards. **Dial**, **Lock**, **Brace**, **Reset**, **Stack**, **Pace**
all made the candidate list.

**Culture / identity.**
Box, athlete, coach, PR, meet, comp, throw-down, ghost record, back-off,
warm-up, cool-down, whiteboard, leaderboard, affiliate, open, quals,
regionals, Games, bar, bumper, kettlebell. **Whiteboard** is the daily
surface every CrossFitter sees but it's a mouthful and Airbnb-derived
brand shapes (Beyond the Whiteboard already exists). *Cut.* **PR** is
what the app helps you set — too generic to own. **Box** is a brand shape
in itself but the word is already Zuckerberg's file-storage company.

**Blade / craft metaphors that live in the gym.**
Sharp, sharpen, hone, whet, edge, notch, chisel, temper, forge, anvil,
dial, dial in, prime, tune, pace, spike, ramp, taper, cut, trim. This is
Terav's home family and it's where round 1 fought. I'm not going to
re-litigate Hone/Temper/Whetstone — they're in the round 1 reports. But
one crossover verb that connects blade-language to gym-language survived
into my list: **Dial**, which is the exact word Chris Hinshaw and Ben
Bergeron use on camera every week.

## Candidates (14)

### 1. Dial

- **Origin.** Coaching cue — "dial it in." Used by Ben Bergeron and every
  strength coach on YouTube. Also engineering: dial in a torque wrench, a
  scope, a tempo.
- **Why it fits.** One syllable, opens on /d/ plosive, closes on liquid /l/
  — clean shout on the box wall ("On Dial this cycle"). The verb is already
  the exact promise: the app dials in the plan on your weakness. Better
  than "Sharpen" on gym-native vocabulary — nobody on the floor says
  "sharpen the plan," but they say "dial it in" every single session.
- **Why it might not.** Dial is a soap brand (Henkel) and *dialpad* owns
  the tech-adjacent SEO. Also short-generic-verb trademark risk is real.
- **CTA.** "Dial the plan tonight." — clean, imperative, native. "Dialled
  in?" as a daily prompt is even better.
- **Domain read.** dial.com is Henkel's soap. dial.fit / dial.app / getdial
  plausible.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Beats Sharpen** on gym-floor vocabulary
  by a wide margin. Every CrossFitter says "dial it in"; nobody says
  "sharpen the plan." Loses to Sharpen only on the transparency-of-Terav
  translation — but the audience doesn't know or care about Terav.

### 2. Rx

- **Origin.** CrossFit prescription notation — "did you do it Rx?" means
  the prescribed version, no scaling. The most-said two letters in the box
  after WOD.
- **Why it fits.** Absolute audience vocabulary. Nobody in the target
  demographic needs it explained. Doubles as promise — the app writes
  your prescription. Two-character monogram is a designer's dream.
- **Why it might not.** Medical connotation (Rx = pharmacy prescription)
  is exactly the medical vibe the brief bans. Also CrossFit HQ has aggressive
  trademark posture on "Rx'd." Also, two-character brands don't survive as
  domains or search terms.
- **CTA.** "Rx the plan tonight." — reads odd; "Rx" is not naturally a verb.
- **Domain read.** dead. rx.com is a pharmacy conglomerate.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Loses to Sharpen.** The medical adjacency
  is fatal for an app that's explicitly rehab-adjacent — users would confuse
  it with a script-tracking tool. Also the trademark ground is claimed.

### 3. Ladder

- **Origin.** CrossFit format — a ladder is an ascending rep or load scheme
  (1-2-3-4-5 pull-ups). Also strength training: 5/3/1 is a ladder, wave
  loading is a ladder. Universal in the audience.
- **Why it fits.** The app *builds* a ladder for you — the whole progressive
  overload thesis is a ladder. Two-syllable trochee (LAD-der), plosive
  close, warm on the ear. "On Ladder this cycle" reads native. Semantic
  connection is strong: you climb it, session by session.
- **Why it might not.** Ladder is a big life-insurance startup (Ladder Life)
  and also a Jake Gyllenhaal fitness app that acquired significant users.
  Direct fitness collision — likely disqualifying.
- **CTA.** "Ladder the plan tonight." — awkward as verb.
- **Domain read.** Ladder.sport is the Gyllenhaal app. Dead.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Loses to Sharpen** on domain contention
  alone — Ladder (the app) is a direct fitness-space collision that
  Sharpen doesn't have.

### 4. Chipper

- **Origin.** CrossFit format — a chipper is a long WOD you "chip away at"
  one rep at a time. Native vocabulary. Also British slang for "cheerful,"
  which is friendly not off-tone.
- **Why it fits.** Two-syllable trochee, hard consonants top and tail,
  playful register — the audience laughs when they hear "today's a chipper"
  because they know it's going to hurt. Semantic connection to the app:
  training is a chipper you work through, one weakness at a time. Ownable —
  no fitness brand has this word.
- **Why it might not.** Chipper is also a wood-chipper (industrial) and a
  fintech startup in Africa (Chipper Cash). Also, the "cheerful" reading
  might feel too soft for an evidence-first, honest brand.
- **CTA.** "Chipper the plan tonight." — reads odd as verb; better as noun
  ("Today's chipper is ready").
- **Domain read.** chippercash.com owns the fintech; chipper.fit / chipper.app
  plausible.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Loses to Sharpen** on verb-forwardness —
  Chipper is a noun in gym vocabulary, not a verb. Sharpen wins the CTA test.

### 5. Engine

- **Origin.** Physiology metaphor — "build your engine" means build your
  aerobic base. Every conditioning coach uses it (Hinshaw, Sevan, the
  Deka franchise). "How's your engine?" is a normal question.
- **Why it fits.** Native vocabulary for the aerobic-domain user. The app
  *is* an engine — the adaptive logic that produces sessions. Two-syllable
  trochee (EN-gine), plosive close, confident on the ear. "On Engine this
  cycle" reads clean and slightly self-aware.
- **Why it might not.** "Engine" is a very common English word (search-engine,
  game-engine) and there are 30+ fitness brands using it (Xero Engine,
  Engine Athletic). Also, the audience uses "engine" for the *aerobic*
  domain specifically — an app that also does strength and rehab might feel
  mis-signalled.
- **CTA.** "Engine the plan tonight." — fails as verb. "Fire up Engine." works.
- **Domain read.** engine.com is CrossFit HQ's Rogue-adjacent territory in
  practice; many fitness collisions. Dead in most slots.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Loses to Sharpen.** Engine reads as a
  domain (aerobic) not a promise; Sharpen reads as a promise. Also verb
  fails.

### 6. Threshold

- **Origin.** Physiology term — lactate threshold, aerobic threshold.
  Coaches and lifters use it daily. Also carries a spatial meaning: the
  threshold of a door, the edge of a change.
- **Why it fits.** The app *finds* your threshold and works you against it —
  the whole personalisation thesis is a threshold thesis. Rich, three-syllable
  word that lifters read as smart, not pretentious. Distinctive.
- **Why it might not.** Three syllables is the wrong count for a brand
  (heavy on the mouth). Also, the word is used more in endurance than in
  CrossFit — off-audience for the strength side. Also several coaching
  brands use it (Threshold Training, Threshold Endurance).
- **CTA.** "Threshold the plan tonight." — fails as verb.
- **Domain read.** threshold.com is a Target home-goods sub-brand. Dead.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Loses to Sharpen.** Syllable count and
  brand distinctiveness both fail.

### 7. Wheelhouse

- **Origin.** CrossFit/coaching term — "your wheelhouse" is the domain
  where you're strong. Every CrossFit gym coach and Games broadcast uses it.
  Rooted in baseball ("in his wheelhouse" = pitch in his hitting zone).
- **Why it fits.** The app is explicitly about the *opposite* of your
  wheelhouse — it finds your weakness — but the name works ironically:
  "Wheelhouse trains outside your wheelhouse." Two-syllable trochee, warm,
  distinctive, evocative. Nobody in fitness apps owns it.
- **Why it might not.** Ironic naming is a step the brief cautions against
  ("no cleverness that requires explanation"). Also long domain, two-syllable
  monogram is a fight, and the metaphor loops back on itself in a way that
  might confuse first-timers.
- **CTA.** "Wheelhouse tonight." — awkward.
- **Domain read.** wheelhouse.com is a hospitality-pricing SaaS. wheelhouse.fit
  might be open but the domain space is contested.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Loses to Sharpen.** Ironic naming is a
  cost that Sharpen doesn't pay.

### 8. Lock

- **Origin.** Coaching imperative — "lock in," "lock it in," "lock your
  midline." Universal cue. Also Olympic lifting: "lock out" (the top of a
  jerk). Wrestling: "lock." Every strength discipline uses it.
- **Why it fits.** One syllable, /k/-plosive close — one of the sharpest
  single-syllable words in English. The verb is imperative and the promise
  is direct: the app locks in your plan on your weakness. Beats Sharpen
  on shoutability by a mile.
- **Why it might not.** "Lock" as a brand name reads slightly mechanical /
  security-adjacent (locks, passwords, LoLK). Also short-common-verb trademark
  risk is bad. "Lock in" as a phrase has recently been colonised by Gen-Z
  meme culture ("I'm locked in") — that could age poorly or could ride the
  wave.
- **CTA.** "Lock the plan tonight." — clean, imperative, punchy. "Lock in
  tonight" is even better as a daily prompt.
- **Domain read.** lock.com is unclear (probably a security or crypto brand);
  lock.app / lock.fit / lockin.app plausible.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Beats Sharpen** on shoutability, on
  gym-floor vocabulary (every coach says "lock in"), and on CTA rhythm.
  Loses on semantic connection to Terav ("lock" isn't "sharp"). But the
  audience doesn't know Terav.

### 9. Brace

- **Origin.** Universal cue in strength — "brace," "brace your midline,"
  "brace against the bar." Also rehab vocabulary: brace an ankle, brace a
  joint. Doubles as anatomical (posture) and mental (brace yourself).
- **Why it fits.** One syllable, /br/ cluster opens hard, /s/ close is
  slightly soft but the word overall snaps. Semantic connection is direct:
  the app braces your plan against your data. Rehab-relevant, strength-
  relevant, aerobic-neutral — covers the full product surface.
- **Why it might not.** Slightly rehab-flavoured (medical brace); might
  read as an injury brand rather than a training brand. Also several
  wellness/PT brands use it.
- **CTA.** "Brace the plan tonight." — reads clean, imperative. "Brace and
  go." as a session-start prompt is native.
- **Domain read.** brace.com is a small dev/security tool. brace.fit /
  brace.app plausible.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Roughly ties Sharpen.** Beats on
  one-syllable-mouth-feel; loses on the "does it say sharpen?" transparency.
  The rehab tilt is either a strength (rehab-adjacent audience) or a weakness
  (over-signals injury), depending on positioning.

### 10. Pace

- **Origin.** Coaching noun and verb — "pace it," "hold pace," "find your
  pace." Universal in aerobic and strength. Also non-athletic English:
  set the pace, put it through its paces.
- **Why it fits.** One syllable, plosive open, /s/ close, warm-mouth
  vowel. The verb is imperative and the promise fits — the app paces you
  session by session. Rehab, aerobic, and strength all use "pace."
- **Why it might not.** Pace is generic — many pacing apps and a running
  brand (Pace magazine, Pace hydration, Runna is *about* pace). Distinctive-
  ness is low. Also "pace" reads slightly more endurance than lift.
- **CTA.** "Pace the plan tonight." — reads slightly odd; you don't "pace"
  a plan. "Pace tonight's session" better.
- **Domain read.** pace.com is a running/hospitality brand. Dead.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Loses to Sharpen** on distinctiveness and
  semantic precision.

### 11. Prime

- **Origin.** Coaching verb — "prime the pump," "prime your CNS," "prime
  the movement pattern." Native strength-and-conditioning vocabulary. Also
  physics/maths (prime number) and adjective (prime condition).
- **Why it fits.** One syllable, /pr/-cluster opens athletic, closes on
  /aɪm/. Gym-native. The app *primes* you for the next session — verb fits.
- **Why it might not.** Amazon Prime. Prime Hydration. Prime Male. Prime
  Video. The word is corporate-owned in every important vertical. SEO is
  cooked.
- **CTA.** "Prime the plan tonight." — reads clean, imperative.
- **Domain read.** dead.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Loses to Sharpen** on domain contention
  alone. Would be strong otherwise.

### 12. Stack

- **Origin.** Gym vocabulary — "stack the plates," "stack the reps," "stack
  another set." Also programming lingo (fitness-adjacent: "stack" your
  training / your habits). Rich Froning uses "stack" constantly. Also
  supplements: "my stack" (compound noun for what you take).
- **Why it fits.** One syllable, plosive close, athletic on the mouth,
  active verb. The app helps you stack your training — one weakness, one
  session, one small gain, stacked. Progressive by nature. Nobody in
  fitness apps owns this word.
- **Why it might not.** "Stack" has heavy tech-adjacency (tech stack,
  Stack Overflow, LAMP stack) which is exactly the tech-VC vibe the brief
  bans. Also "stack" in supplement culture is bro-adjacent.
- **CTA.** "Stack the plan tonight." — clean, imperative, punchy. "Stack
  another one" as an end-of-session prompt is native.
- **Domain read.** stack.com is a design-system tool. Stack Overflow. Many
  Stack-something brands. Dead in the obvious slots.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Loses to Sharpen.** Tech-adjacency is
  fatal for a founder who explicitly wants to avoid startup-tech vibes.

### 13. Reset

- **Origin.** Coaching cue — "reset your position," "reset between reps,"
  "hit reset." Also rehab: "movement reset," "positional reset." Every
  physio and PT uses this. Bergeron and Kelly Starrett both say it weekly.
- **Why it fits.** Two-syllable trochee (RE-set), clean plosive close,
  imperative. Semantic connection: the app resets your plan every week
  based on how the last block landed. Rehab-and-training native. The
  "confirm-first adaptive engine" is literally a reset the user has to
  approve — the name maps to the exact interaction.
- **Why it might not.** Reset is generic in tech (button, factory reset)
  and there are wellness brands (Reset by Dr. Peter Attia — small but
  audience-adjacent). Also, "reset" implies starting over, which
  contradicts the app's progressive-only guarantee.
- **CTA.** "Reset the plan tonight." — reads clean. Might be too close to
  "delete my progress" for a training app.
- **Domain read.** reset.com is unclear; many reset-something apps. reset.fit
  / reset.app plausible.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Loses to Sharpen.** The "starting over"
  connotation contradicts the app's core promise (progressive rule) — this
  is a naming trap.

### 14. Kip (wild-card, CrossFit-history bold pick)

- **Origin.** CrossFit movement — the kip is the hip-swing that makes
  advanced gymnastics work (kipping pull-ups, kipping HSPU, kipping T2B).
  The most argued-about movement in CrossFit history. To "kip" is a verb.
  Rich Froning "kips" his muscle-ups. Everyone in the audience knows it.
- **Why it fits.** One syllable, both plosive (/k/ open, /p/ close),
  distinctive on the ear. Semantic connection is metaphorical but real:
  the kip is the mechanism that converts effort into efficient movement —
  which is what the app does with your training data. Deeply CrossFit-native
  — nobody outside the audience has heard it, everyone inside has strong
  feelings. Beats Sharpen on tribal-vocabulary signalling.
- **Why it might not.** Divisive inside the audience (some purists hate
  kipping). Might read too niche — an app called Kip signals "we're
  CrossFit-only" and shuts out the strength / rehab / running lifters the
  brief targets. Also, it's not obviously "the plan gets sharper."
- **CTA.** "Kip the plan tonight." — reads odd as verb outside the movement
  context. Fails CTA test.
- **Domain read.** kip.com is a Belgian TV show; kip.fit / kip.app worth
  checking; several small "Kip" brands exist.
- **Head-to-head vs Sharpen.** **Loses to Sharpen** on CTA and on
  audience-breadth — but wins on cultural signalling. Include for the
  founder to consider if bold positioning is on the table.

## Shortlist — top 3

### 1. Dial

The strongest CrossFit-vocab challenger to Sharpen. Every coach in the
audience says "dial it in" every week — Ben Bergeron, Chris Hinshaw, Kelly
Starrett, every YouTube conditioning channel. The verb already means what
the product does (adjust with precision toward a target). One syllable,
plosive open, liquid close, clean shout. CTA writes itself ("Dial the plan
tonight"), monogram D is workable, tense test passes ("I dialled today").
The domain fight is real (Henkel soap owns dial.com, Dialpad owns tech-SEO)
but dial.fit / dial.app / getdial paths are plausible and the trademark
class is different. This is the name that meets the audience where they
already are.

### 2. Lock

The most CTA-native option in the deck. "Lock in" is the phrase every
strength coach uses to start a session, and the audience uses it back —
Gen-Z lifters especially have adopted "locked in" as a status. One syllable,
double-plosive, snaps shut on the /k/. Semantic connection is direct (lock
in your plan on your weakness) and CTAs read as native imperative ("Lock
the plan tonight"). Loses to Sharpen only on Terav-translation transparency,
which is a founder-side concern the audience will never feel. Trademark
risk is real (short common verb) but survivable in the fitness class.

### 3. Brace

The rehab-aware pick. "Brace" is the one word that lives natively in all
three of the app's domains — strength coaches say it before every heavy
lift, rehab physios say it for stability, aerobic coaches say it for
midline holds. One syllable, /br/ cluster punchy, semantic connection is
that the app *braces* your plan against your data (against your reality,
against your yesterday). The rehab tilt is either a bug or a feature
depending on how far the founder wants to lean into the rehab-adjacent
audience — for this specific user (post-injury, adaptive-first), it's a
feature. Ties Sharpen roughly; would beat if positioning goes rehab-first.

The three cut with regret: **Wheelhouse** (irony-cost too high but the
metaphor is beautiful), **Engine** (perfect physiology-vocab but verb
fails), **Kip** (bold and tribal but too narrow for the actual audience).

## If I were writing the cheque today

**Dial.** It's the one name in this deck that the audience already speaks
every single week — nobody on any CrossFit floor says "sharpen the plan
tonight," but "dial it in" is background radiation. The verb is *literally*
what the adaptive engine does: it takes your data and dials the load,
volume, and focus toward the target you missed last cycle. It survives every
test the brief asks for — one-syllable shoutability on the box wall
("On Dial this cycle" reads clean), CTA writes itself ("Dial the plan
tonight"), the imperative sits well on a button ("DIAL"), tense test passes,
monogram D is designable (a dial-face D writes itself), and the domain
paths exist below the top-level fight.

Sharpen is the honest founder pick because Terav means sharp and the copy
already uses the word. Dial is the audience pick because the audience is
already saying it. If the goal is a brand that lands faster and gets said
on the box wall without a translation step, Dial is the move. If the goal
is transparency and continuity with Terav's monogram work, Sharpen holds.
My cheque goes to Dial and I would fall back to Lock before falling back
to Sharpen — because between Sharpen and Lock, "Lock in tonight" reads as
a coach's voice and "Sharpen tonight's session" reads as a translated
metaphor.

One caveat worth naming: if the founder is willing to be bold, the
CrossFit-history pick (**Kip**) is the highest-ceiling / highest-risk name
in the vocabulary. It would signal "we know exactly who we are and who this
is for" more loudly than any other option. It would also cost the
strength-only and rehab-only segments of the audience. For the current
brief (broad CrossFit + strength + rehab-adjacent), Dial is the better call.
