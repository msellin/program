# Naming Agent 1: Positioning

Angle: **making something sharper, more refined, more targeted.** The name should carry the same tactile promise Terav does — that the plan gets an edge put on it every session, aimed at the user's specific weakness.

---

## Candidate names

### 1. Hone

**Origin.** Old English *han* (stone). To hone is to sharpen a blade on a fine-grit stone — the final pass, after grinding. Precision, not force.

**Why it fits.** The verb already lives in athletic English ("hone your technique") and carries "small, deliberate refinement" better than "sharpen" itself. Monogram H is heavy, symmetrical, works as an icon. Ties cleanly to the promise: the app doesn't rebuild your plan, it hones it.

**Why it might not.** Hone is short and generic enough that a fitness app called Hone almost certainly exists in some corner of the App Store; the .com is unlikely to be free.

**CTA.** "Hone the plan tonight." "Hone in on your weak side this week." Reads naturally.

**Availability read.** hone.com is a big brand (hormone therapy). Rules it out on its own.

---

### 2. Whet

**Origin.** Old English *hwettan* — to sharpen, to make keen. A whetstone is the coarser cousin of the hone; "whet your appetite" is the idiom that survives in English.

**Why it fits.** Single syllable, physical, unusual enough to be defensible. Metaphorically it's the *start* of the sharpening process — coarse pass first, fine pass later — which matches how the app onboards a lifter and then refines the plan over months. W is a strong monogram letter, angular.

**Why it might not.** Most English readers know "whet" only from "whet your appetite" and may not connect it to blades; requires one line of explanation.

**CTA.** "Whet the plan." Works but feels slightly odd on the box wall.

**Availability read.** whet.app plausibly free; whet.com almost certainly taken.

---

### 3. Edge

**Origin.** Old English *ecg* — the sharpened side of a blade. Also the abstract sense: competitive advantage.

**Why it fits.** Double meaning is a gift: the physical edge of a tool, and the edge you have over yesterday's version of yourself. Every session puts an edge on the plan. E as a monogram is soft but the word carries.

**Why it might not.** Microsoft owns Edge as a browser and the SEO fight is unwinnable. "Get on Edge this cycle" reads ambiguous.

**CTA.** "Put an edge on the plan." Good in copy, awkward as a one-word verb.

**Availability read.** Ruled out at the top level. Compound like "TheEdge" or "EdgeLift" pulls it into the gimmick zone the brief bans.

---

### 4. Whetstone

**Origin.** The stone itself — the tool that does the sharpening.

**Why it fits.** Names the *instrument*, not the action, which is honest — the app is the stone, the user is the blade, training is the friction. Two-syllable, tactile, memorable. Nobody in fitness owns this word.

**Why it might not.** Slightly long for a monogram; W-icon has to do a lot of work. "I'm on Whetstone this cycle" reads more literary than gym.

**CTA.** "Take it to the whetstone." Works as tagline, not as one-word verb.

**Availability read.** whetstone.app or whetstone.fit likely open; whetstone.com is a chocolate company but not fitness-adjacent.

---

### 5. Anvil

**Origin.** The block a smith works metal on. The counter-force that shapes the piece.

**Why it fits.** Anvil is the *base* — the unmoving thing your effort lands on. Metaphor for a program: you swing, it holds. A as a monogram is a mountain letter, iconic. Strength-training audiences read anvils positively without it going full iron-gym.

**Why it might not.** Anvil leans forging/hammering (force) more than sharpening (refinement); slightly off-angle for "personalisation on your weakness." A weapons and outdoor brand already exists.

**CTA.** "The plan meets the anvil tonight." Poetic; not imperative.

**Availability read.** anvil.works is taken (low-code). anvil.fit maybe open.

---

### 6. Flint

**Origin.** The stone that strikes the spark; also historically used to knap sharp tools.

**Why it fits.** One syllable, hard consonants, feels athletic without being gym-bro. Metaphor is *initiation* — every session is a strike. F is a strong monogram. Reads well internationally.

**Why it might not.** Flint (Michigan) has heavy connotations that a name search will surface immediately; also several dev-tools and knife brands use it.

**CTA.** "Flint the plan tonight" doesn't work as a verb. "Strike Flint" as a tagline works but is a second layer.

**Availability read.** flint.com is a payments company; app-level names may be open.

---

### 7. Temper

**Origin.** Latin *temperare* — to mix in proportion, to moderate. In metallurgy: reheat a blade after quenching to make it hard *and* tough, not brittle.

**Why it fits.** Temper is the metallurgical step that maps most precisely to the app's job: you don't just harden, you dial in the balance. Aerobic and strength; load and recovery; push and hold. Two syllables, verb-strong, T monogram inherits from Terav.

**Why it might not.** "Temper" also means mood; "lose your temper" is a competing everyday meaning that might collide in copy.

**CTA.** "Temper the plan tonight." Reads well. "I'm on Temper this cycle" — clean.

**Availability read.** temper.works is a Dutch staffing app; temper.fit / temper.app plausibly open.

---

### 8. Calibre

**Origin.** From Arabic *qalib* via French — the internal diameter of a barrel, then figuratively "the measure of a person." British spelling; "caliber" in US.

**Why it fits.** Carries the exact positioning: *precision fit*. The plan is calibred to you. Also holds "quality" as a secondary meaning. C monogram works.

**Why it might not.** Firearms adjacency is a real problem for a fitness app in the US market; also spelling forks between UK/US.

**CTA.** "Calibre the plan" — works but feels stiff.

**Availability read.** calibre.com is an e-reader (heavy brand); calibre.fit likely open but the association is loud.

---

### 9. Notch

**Origin.** Old French *oche* — a cut, a groove, a mark. Also: "notching up a personal best."

**Why it fits.** Every session is a notch — a small precise cut in the plan. Very short, monosyllabic, tactile. Doubles as the progression metaphor athletes already use ("notch up the weight"). N is a solid monogram.

**Why it might not.** iPhone notch is a competing top-of-mind meaning; also "notch" is casual, may undercut the evidence-based tone.

**CTA.** "Notch up tonight." "Notch the plan." Both work casually.

**Availability read.** notch.com is a live-events production suite (visible brand). notch.fit maybe open.

---

### 10. Kilter

**Origin.** English *out of kilter* — from an old word meaning good condition, proper alignment. To be *in kilter* is to be dialled in.

**Why it fits.** Positive form of a phrase everyone knows negatively; feels like a rediscovery. Matches the "aim, align, target" family cleanly — the plan is *in kilter* with your data. K is a sharp, distinctive monogram.

**Why it might not.** Kilter Board is a well-known climbing training brand (LED-lit training board). Direct fitness-training-tool collision — likely disqualifying.

**CTA.** "Get in kilter tonight." Works.

**Availability read.** Kilter Board owns the mindshare and probably the domain family. Rules it out.

---

### 11. Truen

**Origin.** From *true* as a verb — bicycle mechanics *true* a wheel by adjusting spoke tension until it runs straight. Also woodworking: to true an edge is to make it perfectly flat.

**Why it fits.** True-as-verb is the exact promise: the app trues the plan against your data. One syllable, easy to say, T monogram inherits from Terav's icon work. Not a common brand word.

**Why it might not.** "Truen" isn't a real word yet; requires a moment of "oh, like truing a wheel" for a lifter to get it. Verges on invented-word territory the brief is skeptical of.

**CTA.** "True the plan tonight." (Using the real verb.) Reads clean.

**Availability read.** Truen as a coined mark plausibly open across the board.

---

### 12. Grain

**Origin.** The natural direction of fibres in wood or metal. To work *with the grain* is to work with what's there; to sharpen a blade you follow its grain.

**Why it fits.** Metaphor for personalisation: the app works with *your* grain, not against a template. Warm, tactile, non-tech. G is a rounded, friendly monogram — a counterweight to the harder tool words.

**Why it might not.** Food/agriculture adjacency dominates the word; also "grain" doesn't obviously say "sharpen." Off-angle for the core promise.

**CTA.** "Work with the grain." Nice tagline, weak verb.

**Availability read.** grain.com is a meetings-transcription tool (visible). grain.fit likely open.

---

### 13. Chisel

**Origin.** Old French *cisel* — a bladed hand tool for cutting stone or wood, one strike at a time.

**Why it fits.** Chisel is the tool of *deliberate removal* — you don't add mass, you take away everything that isn't the sculpture. Matches the promise: the app removes the sessions that aren't right for you today. Also inherits the "chisel-stroke gradient" language already in the Terav icon brief.

**Why it might not.** "Chisel" has a physique-culture reading ("chiseled abs") that leans exactly into the gym-bro territory the brief bans. Hard to escape.

**CTA.** "Chisel the plan tonight." Works but risks the abs reading.

**Availability read.** chisel.com is a hardware description language (niche tech). chisel.fit almost certainly triggers gym-brand collisions.

---

### 14. Keen

**Origin.** Old English *cene* — sharp, wise, eager. Sharpness in mind *and* blade.

**Why it fits.** Rare double-meaning: a keen edge and a keen athlete. Warm and precise at once. Monosyllabic, easy internationally. K monogram sharp.

**Why it might not.** KEEN is a global footwear brand — direct athletic-adjacent collision. Also common English word, hard to defend.

**CTA.** "Keen the plan tonight" — awkward as verb.

**Availability read.** KEEN Footwear owns the space. Ruled out.

---

### 15. Tang

**Origin.** The part of a blade that runs into the handle — the structural core that lets the edge do work. Also a taste; also a sharp note.

**Why it fits.** Tang is the *hidden* strength of a knife — the part users don't see but that determines whether the tool holds up. Metaphor for the engine: the adaptive logic under the plan. Short, sharp, T monogram inherits from Terav.

**Why it might not.** "Tang" carries the drink brand and a colloquial sexual reading in some English varieties; also short enough that trademark class collisions are near-certain.

**CTA.** "On Tang this cycle" — reads odd.

**Availability read.** Tang (drink) owns the word in most markets.

---

## Shortlist: top 3

### 1. Hone
Closest one-to-one substitute for Terav's core verb. Reads as sharpening *for refinement*, not sharpening *for aggression* — which is exactly the right register for a rehab-aware, evidence-based app. The verb already exists in athletic English so no explainer is needed, and the CTA writes itself. Only fails on brand collision at the .com level; if we can live with hone.fit or hone.app, this is the answer.

### 2. Temper
The most thematically precise name on the list. Tempering is the metallurgical step that balances hardness and toughness — the exact language a lifter would want for an app that decides between *push* and *hold* every day. Inherits the T monogram directly from Terav so the existing icon work is not wasted. Slight risk from the "mood" meaning, but the training reading is strong enough to carry.

### 3. Whetstone
The most defensible mark of the fifteen. Two syllables, tactile, nobody in fitness owns it, unambiguous in meaning once said. Slight loss on monogram economy vs. one-syllable options, but the payoff is a name that can survive as a brand for a decade without collisions.

The others fall out because they either collide with a bigger brand in athletic-adjacent categories (Edge, Keen, Kilter, Calibre, Chisel), lean the wrong direction on tone (Anvil, Flint push force over refinement; Chisel and Grain drift off-angle), or drift into invented-word territory (Truen, Tang).

---

## If I were writing the cheque

**Temper.** It carries the exact metaphor the product is built on — not just "make it sharper" but "balance hardness and toughness for the piece in front of you" — which is what an adaptive engine actually does when it reads a morning check and moves the load. It keeps the T monogram, keeps the "one physical verb" DNA that made Terav strong, and it survives on the box wall: "I'm on Temper this cycle" is a sentence a lifter says without a second thought. Hone is the safer pick, but Temper is the truer one.
