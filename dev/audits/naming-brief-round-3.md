# Naming — Round 3 shared brief

## The linguistic problem to solve

The founder is Estonian. Product will launch in both Estonian and English
markets. Previous rounds landed on **Sharpen** as the founder's preferred
English name and considered keeping **Terav** as the brand mark with
"sharpen the plan" as a permanent tagline. Both of those have a hidden
problem.

**In English**, "sharpen the plan / sharpen the skill / sharpen the session"
reads native. The verb *sharpen* metaphorises skill-improvement everywhere.

**In Estonian**, "terav" (sharp) does NOT metaphorise the way "sharp" does
in English. To describe polishing/refining a skill you say **lihvida** (to
polish, refine, from *lihv* = grind, polish, chamfer). "Terita" (imperative
of "teritada" = to sharpen a blade) is used for blades and pencils, not for
skills. So all natural Estonian copy would use **lihvi** or **viimistle**
(refine, finish) — words that have nothing to do with the brand mark
Terav.

That means the "brand IS the verb" story falls apart in Estonian.
CTAs, hero sections, and imperative buttons can't natively use Terav as a
verb. The Estonian copy would have to bridge:
- "Terita oma plaani." — grammatical but reads like an English calque
- "Lihvi oma plaani." — natural Estonian, but has nothing to do with the
  brand name

This was the founder's original hesitation about branding on Terav.
Sharpen (in English) makes it worse because now BOTH languages have a
brand name that doesn't naturally verb in the other market.

## What the founder actually wants

- A brand name that either
  (a) **works naturally in both languages as a verb / imperative**, OR
  (b) works as a brand mark in both languages and doesn't force awkward
      translated CTAs — where "kasutan Slacki" (I use Slack) works for
      Slack in Estonian because Slack doesn't try to also be the verb
- The Estonian-market copy must not feel like calque or translated English
- The English-market copy still needs the verb-forward feel we've been
  building for
- The T-monogram investment (bronze chisel-stroke) is a nice-to-keep but
  not a blocker

## Estonian verbs / roots for skill improvement (for reference)

- **lihvima / lihvi** — to polish, to grind, to refine (most natural for
  "improve a skill"). Root: **lihv** (noun) = grind, polish, chamfer.
- **teritama / terita** — to sharpen a blade / pencil. Root: **tera**
  (blade). This is what Terav is built on but the verb only works for
  physical objects in Estonian.
- **viimistlema / viimistle** — to finish, refine (surface finish, final
  polish). Longer, formal.
- **arendama / arenda** — to develop. Generic.
- **täiustama / täiusta** — to perfect. Too aspirational.
- **sättima / sätti** — to adjust, arrange. Casual, native.
- **kohandama** — to adapt. Corporate.
- **treenima / treeni** — to train. Universal athletic verb but too
  generic to name after.

## What the two round-3 agents need to explore

Each agent takes a distinct angle.

### Agent 1: Estonian-first
Should we pick a name from an Estonian root that also travels in English?
Options include:
- **Lihv** — the Estonian noun for polish/grind. Short, clean, unusual
  monogram L. Translates as a natural CTA in Estonian ("lihvi plaani").
  Reads foreign in English but so does "Whoop."
- **Sätt** — from "sättima" (adjust). Short, phonetic, close to
  "set/setting" in English.
- **Serv** — Estonian for "edge". Two-letter monogram.
- Any others.
Also test whether **Terav should be kept with a permanent Estonian tagline
("Terav — lihvi plaani")** matching how it'd work in English.

### Agent 2: Bilingual-native
Should we pick a short English word whose meaning-carries into Estonian
via a loan / cognate? Options include:
- **Focus** — Estonian uses "fookus" as a loan word. CTA "Fokusseeri
  plaani" works but is corporate.
- **Prime** — Estonian doesn't verb it.
- **Peak** — "Peak" is a loanword some Estonians use in sports.
- **Dial** — no Estonian equivalent.
- Words with Latin/Nordic roots that carry across.
- Or: **an entirely coined name** (like Slack, Notion, Whoop) that both
  languages just say without needing to verb it.
