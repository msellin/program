# Naming — Round 3, Agent 2: Bilingual-Native

Angle: **The brand name has to survive as a marketing artefact in Estonian
copy AND English copy, without either side reading like a translation.**

The founder's insight I'm building on: a name whose *meaning is a verb in
one language but not the other* is a compromise, because the CTA in the
other language is either a calque (unnatural) or a bridge (loses the
brand-verb identity). Sharpen breaks in Estonian because Estonians don't
metaphorise blade-sharpening for skill work — they say **lihvi** or
**viimistle**. Terav breaks in English for the mirror reason — nobody says
"Terav the plan tonight" without an explainer.

So I'm working three strategies below. **A. The Slack model** — the brand
is a proper noun, neither language expects it to also be the verb, both
languages inherit natural verbs around it. **B. The verb-in-both-languages
hunt** — genuinely bilingual verbs (rare, mostly loan roots). **C. The
Estonian tech-brand precedent** — Estonian founders overwhelmingly name in
English with short common English words (Bolt, Wise, Skype, Pipedrive),
because the Estonian market accepts English brand marks without needing
the mark to also be an Estonian verb.

The ghost in every head-to-head is **Sharpen**, the current founder pick.

---

## Ground-truth on the "verb in both languages" question

Before candidates: does any word truly work as a native verb in BOTH
English and Estonian without one side sounding translated? I tested the
short list of international-Latin loans that the brief nominated plus the
obvious extras:

| Word | English verb? | Estonian verb form | Reads native in Estonian? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Focus | yes ("focus the plan") | fokusseerima → "fokusseeri plaani" | corporate, four syllables, clunky — no |
| Prime | yes ("prime the plan") | no native verb; "praimima" is slang, tech, marginal | no |
| Peak | yes but weak ("peak the plan" fails) | "peakima" not a word | no |
| Tune | yes ("tune the plan") | "tuunima" exists (car-tuning slang) | slang register, not native |
| Test | yes ("test the plan") | testima → "testi plaani" | generic, works, but too generic to brand |
| Programm | weak | programmeerima | too generic and coder-adjacent |
| Sync | yes ("sync the plan") | sünkima / sünkroniseerima | tech register, not gym |
| Load | yes ("load the plan") | "laadima plaani" is possible | reads as "download/charge," ambiguous |
| Snap | yes ("snap the plan") | no native verb | no |
| Kick | yes ("kick the plan") | "kickima" is slang football | no |
| Draft | yes ("draft the plan") | no verb; "draftima" is poker slang | no |
| Cadence | noun | "kadents" is a noun only | no verb |
| Beat | yes ("beat the plan") | "biidima" not a word | no |
| Torque | noun | ei ole verb | no |
| Pace | verb but weak | "peisima" not a word | no |

**Verdict on Strategy B:** no international loan works as a natural,
non-corporate verb in both languages. The Estonian verb-suffix -ima can be
glued onto any noun (programmima, laadima, tuunima), but the result is
either slang, tech-jargon, or corporate. The founder's instinct was
correct: verb-in-both is a trap. The strong play is Strategy A (Slack
model) or Strategy C (Estonian-founder English pick). Candidates below
lean there.

## Candidates (14)

### 1. Kova (Strategy A + Estonian-Finnic bridge)

- **Origin.** Estonian **kõva** = "hard, strong, solid" (colloquially:
  "kõva mees" = a strong/tough man). Also Finnish **kova** = "hard." The
  vowel differs (**õ** vs **o**) but the roots are cognate and Estonians
  read the "kova" spelling as the international/Finnish variant.
- **Slack-model check.** The brand is a proper noun. Estonian speakers
  don't expect it to also be the verb; English speakers don't expect it
  to mean anything. Estonian CTAs use natural Estonian verbs: "Lihvi
  plaani Kovaga," "Ava täna Kova." English CTAs use natural English:
  "Open Kova," "Kova sharpens the plan."
- **English CTA test.** "Open Kova tonight." / "Kova the plan tonight" —
  the second reads oddly, the first reads clean; brand as proper noun.
- **Estonian CTA test.** "Lihvi plaani Kovaga." / "Ava täna Kova." — both
  natural; the brand doesn't wrestle with the verb.
- **Bilingual verdict.** Works in both. Estonians hear a tough-sounding
  familiar word; English speakers hear a short invented-feel brand
  (Kia, Nokia, Skype-adjacent). The **õ/o** slippage is a positive: the
  brand transliterates cleanly for export.
- **Trademark / domain read.** kova.com is unclear (small brands
  contest); kova.fit / kova.app / usekova plausible.
- **vs Sharpen.** **Beats Sharpen** on Estonian-side naturalness because
  the CTA is now "lihvi plaani Kovaga" (native Estonian verb, Kova as
  brand), not "terita plaani" (calque). Loses on English-side
  transparency (Sharpen means what it says; Kova has to be learned).

### 2. Lihv (Strategy A — Estonian noun, exported as brand)

- **Origin.** Estonian noun **lihv** = polish, grind, chamfer, finish.
  Root of **lihvima** = "to polish/refine a skill." The most-used Estonian
  verb for skill improvement.
- **Slack-model check.** In Estonian, the brand and the verb-family are
  related but distinct — the brand is the noun (Lihv), the verb is
  "lihvima." Estonians read this the same way English speakers read
  "Grind" as a brand: adjacent, resonant, not identical. In English,
  Lihv is a proper noun with no meaning, like Whoop or Grindr.
- **English CTA test.** "Open Lihv tonight." / "Lihv the plan tonight" —
  first works, second sounds odd because English readers can't verb it.
- **Estonian CTA test.** "Lihvi oma plaani Lihviga." — this is the trap.
  The verb form of the app name is the exact word for the action, which
  reads punny at best and confusing at worst ("polish your plan with
  Polish"). Better: "Ava Lihv. Lihvi plaani." — but the doubling still
  echoes.
- **Bilingual verdict.** The Estonian side is strong but the noun-verb
  proximity creates a pun the founder will hate after month two. In
  English, "Lihv" has the pronunciation problem Terav has ("LIV"? "LIF"?
  "LEEF"?).
- **Trademark / domain read.** lihv.com and lihv.ee are likely open —
  small Estonian tooling brand risk only.
- **vs Sharpen.** **Ties Sharpen.** Wins Estonian-side (natural
  metaphor), loses English-side (unpronounceable), and the noun-verb
  pun is a wash.

### 3. Serv (Strategy A — Estonian "edge")

- **Origin.** Estonian **serv** = edge (of a table, blade, page).
  International cognate: English "serve," Latin "servus."
- **Slack-model check.** Estonian speakers would read "Serv" as the noun
  "edge" — closely related to Terav semantically. English speakers read
  it as "serve" (a tennis serve, a server, a restaurant term).
- **English CTA test.** "Serv the plan tonight." — reads like a typo of
  "serve." Fails.
- **Estonian CTA test.** "Lihvi plaani Serviga." — grammatical but the
  brand carries "edge" connotation without solving the verb problem.
- **Bilingual verdict.** English collision with "serve" (server, service,
  restaurant) is fatal for a training brand. Cut.
- **vs Sharpen.** **Loses.** Homophone collision with English "serve" is
  too costly.

### 4. Kalju (Strategy A — Estonian, exported)

- **Origin.** Estonian **kalju** = cliff, rock. Common Estonian first
  name (Kalju), tough/steady connotation.
- **Slack-model check.** Estonian: proper name / noun, doesn't try to
  verb. English: invented-feel two-syllable brand (Kalju, Baiju,
  Skype-adjacent).
- **English CTA test.** "Open Kalju tonight." — works as brand;
  pronunciation ambiguous for English readers (KAL-yoo).
- **Estonian CTA test.** "Lihvi plaani Kaljuga." — clean, native.
- **Bilingual verdict.** Estonian-clean, English-uncertain-pronunciation.
  The English side needs a phonetic helper.
- **vs Sharpen.** **Loses.** Pronunciation ambiguity is the exact problem
  Terav has; renaming to another mispronounceable Estonian word is a
  lateral move.

### 5. Skope (Strategy A — invented, Greek root)

- **Origin.** Invented from Greek **skopein** = "to look, aim, examine"
  (root of scope, telescope, microscope). One-syllable, ends on
  plosive-cluster /p/.
- **Slack-model check.** Neither language expects Skope to be a verb.
  Estonian CTAs use lihvi/viimistle; English CTAs use aim/sharpen/tune.
  Brand sits on top as a proper noun.
- **English CTA test.** "Open Skope. Sharpen the plan tonight." — brand
  and verb decoupled cleanly.
- **Estonian CTA test.** "Ava Skope. Lihvi plaani." — clean, native
  Estonian imperatives on either side of the brand mark.
- **Bilingual verdict.** Works in both. Greek root is a shared cultural
  substrate; Estonians read it as international, English speakers read
  it as adjacent to "scope" (aim, focus, target — all training-adjacent
  meanings).
- **Trademark / domain read.** skope.com is a NZ refrigeration brand,
  category-distant. skope.fit / skope.app plausible.
- **vs Sharpen.** **Beats Sharpen.** Estonian CTAs go native ("lihvi
  plaani") without dragging the brand into a verb-that-doesn't-work.
  English CTAs go native ("sharpen the plan") without renaming. The
  brand carries both.

### 6. Notio (Strategy A — invented, Latin root)

- **Origin.** From Latin **notio** = "concept, notion" (root of English
  "notion"). Two-syllable, trochee, feminine ending.
- **Slack-model check.** Neither language verbs it. Both languages
  recognise the Latin root.
- **English CTA test.** "Open Notio. Sharpen the plan." — clean.
- **Estonian CTA test.** "Ava Notio. Lihvi plaani." — clean.
- **Bilingual verdict.** Works in both. Estonian speakers will
  pronounce "NO-tsee-oh" and English speakers "NO-tee-oh"; slight
  variance but both are legible.
- **Trademark / domain read.** Notion (Ivan Zhao's productivity app) is
  the collision. Notio.com plausible but "Notio" reads as a Notion
  spin-off. Fails distinctiveness.
- **vs Sharpen.** **Loses.** Notion-adjacency is too close for a
  distinctive brand play.

### 9. Spike (Strategy C — Estonian-tech English pick)

- **Origin.** English noun/verb — a peak, a sudden increase; native to

  (spike your heart rate). Estonian speakers use "spike" in tech and
  volleyball ("spike'ida" is slang).
- **Slack-model check.** English verbs it ("spike the plan"). Estonian
  loans it as a noun ("piik" is the direct Estonian, but "spike" is
  used in sports slang).
- **English CTA test.** "Spike the plan tonight." — reads odd; you
  spike a metric, not a plan.
- **Estonian CTA test.** "Lihvi plaani Spike'iga." — grammatical but
  the apostrophe-i is a red flag (English brand + Estonian case).
- **Bilingual verdict.** English semantic mismatch ("spike" implies
  overload, opposite of adaptive-and-progressive). Cut.
- **vs Sharpen.** **Loses.** Semantic mismatch (spike = overload,
  Sharpen = refine).

## Shortlist — top 3

### 1. Kova

The strongest Strategy-A pick. Estonians hear "kõva" resonance — a
familiar tough/strong word without the exact orthography. Finns hear
their own word. English speakers hear an invented-feel two-syllable
brand adjacent to Nokia, Kia, Skype. Neither market expects Kova to
be a verb: Estonian CTAs use **lihvi plaani** natively; English CTAs
use **sharpen the plan** natively; the brand sits on top of both,
Slack-style. The bronze T-monogram doesn't transfer (K would need
work), but a chisel-stroke K on the same warm-dark palette is
designable. Kova's semantic ("hard, strong, solid") complements the
adaptive-training register better than a "sharp" brand does —
Estonians don't need a knife metaphor; they respond to
strength/toughness metaphors, and the app is fundamentally about
building resilient capacity, not just refining a technique. Trademark
math is workable in fitness Class 9/41.

### 2. Skope

The strongest Strategy-A pick from the Greek-root direction. One
syllable in English (SKOHP), two in Estonian (SKO-pe), plosive
close, easy in either mouth. Neither language expects it to verb —
which is the whole point. Estonian CTAs go native ("lihvi plaani,"
"viimistle plaani," "sätti plaani") because the brand doesn't try
to *be* the verb; it *hosts* the verb. English CTAs go native
("sharpen the plan," "dial the plan," "aim tonight's session")
for the same reason. The Greek root **skopein** = "to look, aim,
examine" is a shared cultural substrate that both English and
Estonian readers respect — you don't need to explain it. It also
lets the app's promise-language stay verbable in both markets
without the brand ever competing with the verb. Domain math is
workable (skope.com is a category-distant refrigeration brand;
skope.fit / skope.app plausible), and monogram S on a
chisel-stroke is designable.

### 3. Notch

The bilingual-safe carry-over from Round 2. Short English word,
Estonians accept it as a foreign brand mark (no verb pressure).
Estonian CTAs use **lihvi plaani** cleanly; English CTAs use
**notch the plan** with a progression-marking semantic that's
already native to gym vocabulary ("notch a PR"). Loses on
semantic precision (notching = marking, sharpening = refining)
but wins on brevity and on bilingual cleanliness. If the founder
wants to keep the Round 2 semantic conversation on the table,
Notch is the one that also survives the round-3 bilingual gate.

Cut with regret: **Lihv** (Estonian-natural but English
unpronounceable; noun-verb pun with lihvima), **Draft** (bilingually
clean, SEO cooked by DraftKings), **Nudge** (bilingually clean,
semantic underplays scope).

## If I were writing the cheque

**Kova.** Sharpen was the right instinct — every CTA is already the
verb, the brand IS the promise. But in Estonian, that trick breaks:
Estonians don't sharpen skills, they polish them (lihvida). Any
Estonian marketing copy would either calque ("terita plaani") or
bridge ("Sharpen — lihvi oma plaani"). The bridge is a compromise the
founder will hate.

Kova stops fighting the bilingual verb war. It says: the brand is a
proper noun both languages inherit, the verb belongs to each language
natively, and the copy in each market reads as if it was written
first. English marketing writes "Sharpen the plan with Kova"; Estonian
marketing writes "Lihvi plaani Kovaga." Neither sentence is a
translation of the other; both are native. That's what Slack got right,
what Bolt got right, what Wise got right. Every one of them is an
English word or an invented word, none of them try to also be the
verb in Estonian — and Estonians say "Kasutan Slacki" without a
second thought.

Kova adds two things a Strategy-C pure-English pick (Notch, Draft,
Grade) doesn't: (a) Estonian resonance — kõva is a familiar tough
word, the brand has *some* home-market meaning without demanding
verb duty from it; (b) semantic complement — the app builds
resilience and capacity, and "hard/strong/solid" says that better
than "sharp" does for a rehab-adjacent user rebuilding after injury.
The founder's whole reason for building this app was the anterior-hip
rebuild. Naming it after strength and durability, not just sharpness,
matches the story better.

If the founder rejects Estonian-flavoured branding entirely and wants
a clean Strategy-A international pick, **Skope** is the answer —
Greek-root, one syllable in English, bilingually neutral, verb-free.
If the founder wants the Round 2 conversation to hold and only wants
the Estonian-verb problem fixed, **Notch** is the answer — English
brand, both markets accept it, natural verbs live on either side.

The one thing I would not do: keep Sharpen. It works beautifully in
English and produces a calque in Estonian every time — and this app
launches to fifty Estonians before it launches to anyone else. The
first fifty users deserve copy that reads native. Kova gives them
that without giving anything up in English.
