# Naming Agent 2 — Linguistic Feel

Angle: phonetics, memorability, how the name lives in speech. Every candidate
is stress-tested against the box, the Instagram bio, the plosive, the syllable,
the airport-page, the trainer-say, the pronunciation gate, the Nordic/Latin
lookalike, and the bad-baggage checks. I'm ranking on how a name FEELS on the
ear, not on how clever it is on paper.

Phonetic principles I applied:

- **Plosives before fricatives.** /k/, /t/, /p/, hard /g/ carry across a
  crowded room. /s/, /f/, /v/, /ʃ/ soften and get eaten by ambient noise.
- **Stress on the first syllable.** Trochees ("HO-ne", "AN-vil") sound
  athletic. Iambs ("a-DAPT") sound corporate.
- **Front vowels before back vowels for impact words.** /i/ and /e/ cut
  ("Nike", "Whoop"). Rounded back vowels ("moo", "law") slow the mouth down.
- **Consonant clusters at the edges.** Nike ends on the /k/. Strava starts
  on /str/. Both create a hard silhouette on the tongue.
- **Two syllables is the sweet spot.** One-syllable names are strong but
  domain-hostile. Three-syllable names get nicknamed by users. Four is dead.

## Candidates

### 1. Hone

- **What / where from.** English verb, Old English *hān* — to sharpen a
  blade against a whetstone. Direct semantic replacement for "Terav."
- **Why it fits.** One syllable, opens on aspirated /h/ and closes on the
  long /oʊn/ — the mouth shape is a lean forward and a settle back, which is
  exactly the rhythm of a training session. It's already a verb in the
  audience's vocabulary; no explainer needed. Plosive test: fails on hard
  consonants but wins on cleanness — the /n/ closure is clean enough for
  airport-page.
- **Why it might not.** Extremely short → domain will be a fight; probably
  hone.app or hone.fit at best. Also, "Hone" is already a mid-size executive-
  coaching startup (honehq.com) — not fitness, but adjacent.
- **In a CTA.** "Hone the plan tonight." — reads native. This is the CTA
  the brief describes, verbatim.
- **Domain read.** hone.com taken (Hone HQ). hone.fit / hone.app / usehone
  plausible.

### 2. Whet

- **What / where from.** English verb — to sharpen; "whet the appetite,"
  "whetstone." Anglo-Saxon, monosyllabic.
- **Why it fits.** Plosive close on /t/ — snaps shut. Front vowel /ɛ/ keeps
  it bright. On the ear it lives in the same room as "sweat" and "get" —
  physical, English, working-class-verb, not tech. The monogram W has visual
  weight matching the current bronze T. Airport-page test: four letters,
  reads in 0.4 seconds.
- **Why it might not.** Rhymes with "wet," which is bland at best and
  slightly unappealing next to sweat — bad-baggage check gives me pause.
- **In a CTA.** "Whet the plan tonight." — grammatical, but slightly
  literary. Better as "Whet your edge."
- **Domain read.** whet.app, whet.fit likely open; whet.com very likely
  taken by something.

### 3. Anvil

- **What / where from.** English/Germanic. The block you shape metal on —
  the surface that receives the hammer. The training-app-as-anvil metaphor
  is: your data is the anvil, every session shapes you against it.
- **Why it fits.** Trochee (AN-vil), plosive /v/ into clean /l/, front-vowel
  open, hard-consonant close. Reads clean in EN, DE (*Amboss* is different
  but *Anvil* is legible), and Nordic contexts. Confidence-high on the ear —
  it sounds like a piece of equipment, not a wellness app. Passes airport
  test (5 letters, familiar shape). Box test: "I'm on Anvil this cycle" —
  sounds like a program, not a brand.
- **Why it might not.** The metaphor is receive-and-shape, not
  sharpen-and-refine — small semantic drift from Terav. Also several small
  software products called Anvil exist (anvil.works — Python web apps).
- **In a CTA.** "Add Anvil to your morning routine." — natural. "Anvil
  built you this session." — reads like a coach.
- **Domain read.** anvil.com and anvil.works taken. anvil.fit / anvil.app
  worth checking; getanvil.io is out.

### 4. Kilter

- **What / where from.** English idiom — "in kilter" / "out of kilter,"
  meaning proper alignment. Also the name of a well-known climbing
  training board (Kilter Board), which is an audience overlap but not a
  collision.
- **Why it fits.** Two syllables, trochee, opens on /k/ — a plosive so
  clean it's practically a whip crack. "Kilter" sits perfectly on the tongue
  and the /lt/ cluster in the middle gives it structural bite. The plosive
  test is a straight win. The word MEANS alignment against a target, which
  is what the adaptive engine actually does.
- **Why it might not.** Kilter Board's brand recognition is significant in
  the CrossFit-adjacent climbing world — real risk of "isn't that the
  board?" confusion.
- **In a CTA.** "Kilter the plan tonight." — grammatical only if we accept
  it as a verb, which "in kilter" almost supports. "Get in kilter today."
  works better.
- **Domain read.** kilter.com taken (adjacent). kilter.fit / kilter.app
  worth a look.

### 5. Prime

- **What / where from.** English adjective/verb — peak state, or to
  prepare a surface / a pump / a person for work.
- **Why it fits.** One syllable, opens on /pr/ cluster (very athletic —
  same family as "press," "prep," "prime"), closes on /aɪm/ — long open
  vowel, confident. Verb form is native English: "prime the pump," "prime
  your nervous system." The concept fits the app: every session primes you
  for the next.
- **Why it might not.** Amazon Prime owns this word in commerce; SEO is
  catastrophic. Also very common in the fitness space (many Prime-something
  brands). Fails the "must not obviously overlap" test.
- **In a CTA.** "Prime the plan tonight." — good rhythm.
- **Domain read.** dead. Every combination is either Amazon-adjacent or
  taken.

### 6. Edge

- **What / where from.** English — the sharpened side of a blade; a
  competitive advantage.
- **Why it fits.** One syllable, closes on the /dʒ/ affricate — one of the
  most percussive sounds in English, better than /k/ for a name because it
  has both stop and release. Semantically parallel to "sharp" — the edge
  IS what gets sharpened. The Instagram bio "@edge" or "@onedge" reads
  athletic and confident. Passes plosive test with a special weapon (/dʒ/
  affricate), passes trainer-say ("Add Edge to your Tuesday").
- **Why it might not.** Ford Edge, Microsoft Edge, U2's guitarist, and
  approximately 400 fitness brands are called Edge. Airport-page WINS but
  brand-distinctiveness LOSES.
- **In a CTA.** "Sharpen your edge tonight." — works beautifully as a
  tagline. The app doesn't need to BE Edge; edge is what it produces.
  **Recommendation: use "edge" in the tagline, not the name.**
- **Domain read.** dead.

### 7. Keener

- **What / where from.** English — "keen" (sharp, eager) + -er nominaliser.
  Also a light-comic Canadian/British word for "an over-enthusiastic
  student," which I'm choosing to reclaim because the audience IS enthusiastic
  and would find it funny.
- **Why it fits.** Two syllables, opens on hard /k/, front vowel /iː/, closes
  clean on /ər/. On the ear it's confident and slightly playful — which is
  where good CrossFit branding lives (Rogue, Wodify, Beyond the Whiteboard
  all have a wink). "I'm on Keener" reads self-aware and warm, not
  self-serious.
- **Why it might not.** The "over-enthusiastic student" connotation is a
  real hazard in US/UK markets where it lands as slightly diminishing. Also
  domain almost certainly gone.
- **In a CTA.** "Keener sharpened your plan overnight." — reads native and
  a little charming.
- **Domain read.** keener.com almost certainly taken. usekeener /
  keener.fit worth checking.

### 8. Krux (styled Krux, spoken "crux")

- **What / where from.** Latin *crux* — the crux, the crucial point. Also
  climbing vocabulary for the hardest move on a route. Styling with K makes
  it a brand mark rather than the English common noun.
- **Why it fits.** One syllable, opens on hard /k/ (plosive test: win),
  ends on /ks/ cluster (double consonant close: extra bite). Semantically it
  points at the single crucial thing — which is the app's whole thesis
  (find your weakness, work it). Reads confident in EN, DE, Nordic. Latin
  root gives it a Nike/Nasa monogram lineage.
- **Why it might not.** Krux was a big adtech company (acquired by
  Salesforce) so trademark and SEO are contested. The K-for-C swap is a
  slight tech-startup tell.
- **In a CTA.** "Krux built your session." — punchy. "The crux of your
  plan tonight." — even better.
- **Domain read.** krux.com is Salesforce; krux.fit / usekrux plausible.

### 9. Tempo

- **What / where from.** Italian/English — pace, rhythm, timing. Universal
  music and sport vocabulary. In lifting, "tempo work" is a term of art.
- **Why it fits.** Two syllables, trochee (TEM-po), opens on /t/ plosive,
  clean /m/ pivot, closes on open /oʊ/ — sounds like a metronome, which is
  what it is. Passes every reader test in every European language. Passes
  airport-page. On the box: "I'm on Tempo this cycle" reads perfectly
  natural for a strength athlete because tempo IS a training variable.
- **Why it might not.** Tempo is a $250M home-gym brand (tempo.fit). Full
  collision. Fails the "must not obviously overlap" test.
- **In a CTA.** "Tempo tuned tonight's session for you." — beautiful, but
  we can't have it.
- **Domain read.** dead.

### 10. Grit

- **What / where from.** English — small hard particles; also perseverance
  (Angela Duckworth's book). Widely adopted in athletic vocabulary.
- **Why it fits.** One syllable, opens on /gr/ cluster (heavy, physical,
  earthy), closes on /t/ plosive. Sounds like effort. Semantically overlaps
  with grinding/perseverance narrative that CrossFit already speaks.
- **Why it might not.** Ambient in fitness — dozens of Grit-something
  brands, several apps. Also "grit" leans hard into the "grind more"
  narrative the app is explicitly against (personalisation, not more
  volume). Slight bad-baggage.
- **In a CTA.** "Grit picked your session." — reads OK, feels generic.
- **Domain read.** dead in obvious slots.

### 11. Forge

- **What / where from.** English/French — the fire where metal is shaped;
  also the verb meaning to form under heat and pressure.
- **Why it fits.** One syllable, opens on /f/ (soft — a phonetic weakness)
  but closes on /dʒ/ affricate (percussive — a phonetic strength). The
  metaphor is strong and the audience already uses it ("forge your body,"
  "forged in the box"). Reads confident, Nordic-friendly, Latin-legible.
- **Why it might not.** Extremely crowded — CrossFit Forge is a common gym
  name, Forge magazine exists, forge-something apps abound. Distinctiveness
  is low.
- **In a CTA.** "Forge tonight's session." — reads native but has that
  forge-your-destiny cliché aftertaste.
- **Domain read.** dead in obvious slots.

### 12. Nock

- **What / where from.** English archery term — the notch on an arrow that
  seats against the bowstring. To "nock an arrow" is to prepare the shot.
- **Why it fits.** One syllable, opens on /n/ (soft) but closes on hard /k/
  plosive — one of the sharpest single-syllable words in English. The
  metaphor is precise: nocking is the moment of alignment before release,
  which is what the daily session review is. Passes airport-page (four
  letters, distinctive). Passes trainer-say. Passes Nordic lookalike test
  cleanly. Nock is a REAL verb the audience-adjacent (archery, hunting) will
  recognise instantly, and everyone else will parse from context.
- **Why it might not.** Some readers will hear "knock" — slight
  pronunciation gate concern (though it's a homophone, not an ambiguity, so
  I'm keeping it in). Also less obviously connected to lifting than to
  archery / precision sport.
- **In a CTA.** "Nock tonight's session." — reads like a coach. "Nock and
  release." — potential tagline.
- **Domain read.** nock.com may be a small company. nock.fit / nock.app
  plausible.

### 13. Krisp

- **What / where from.** English "crisp" restyled with K — the sound of a
  fresh, sharp, high-fidelity signal. Slight synaesthetic borrow: crisp
  audio, crisp movement, crisp form.
- **Why it fits.** One syllable, opens on /kr/ (hard plosive plus liquid,
  very athletic), closes on /sp/ cluster (double-consonant close). Reads
  fast, sounds precise. The concept "your training, crisp" fits the
  personalisation thesis — noise removed, signal amplified.
- **Why it might not.** Krisp.ai is a well-known noise-cancellation app —
  significant collision. Also the K-for-C stylisation is a tech tell.
- **In a CTA.** "Krisp cleaned up tonight's session." — cute, maybe too
  cute.
- **Domain read.** krisp.ai taken; krisp.fit possibly open.

### 14. Halo (long-shot monogram play)

- **What / where from.** English/Latin *halōs* — a ring of light; the mark
  of achievement (halo effect). Framed here as a monogram-brand in the Nike
  / Nasa lineage — a common word promoted to a brand mark by consistent
  use.
- **Why it fits.** Two syllables (HAY-lo), trochee, opens on aspirated /h/,
  closes on /oʊ/. Reads clean in every European language. Airport-page:
  perfect. Instagram-bio: perfect. Confidence on the ear: high. The halo is
  what the athlete earns — a small, bright, deserved thing.
- **Why it might not.** Microsoft's game franchise owns this word for a
  generation; also many wellness/beauty brands. Distinctiveness LOSES; feel
  WINS.
- **In a CTA.** "Halo picked tonight's session." — feels a shade too
  spiritual. "Earn your halo." — good tagline, wrong app.
- **Domain read.** dead.

### 15. Kest (long-shot Latinate monogram — my most speculative pick)

- **What / where from.** Coined. Draws on Latin *castus* (pure, refined) and
  Old English *cest* (best), shortened to a monogram-shaped mark. Justified
  as a Nike/Ikea-style promotion of a short sound to a brand: no baggage,
  no dictionary entry, no collisions, phonetically engineered.
- **Why it fits.** One syllable, opens on hard /k/ plosive, front vowel
  /ɛ/, closes on /st/ cluster — three-consonant close. The mouth shape is
  exactly the mouth shape of "test" and "best" — words the audience uses
  daily. Zero ambiguity in pronunciation across EN, DE, Nordic (all say
  it the same way). Airport-page: four letters, distinctive silhouette,
  strong K monogram. Instagram bio: @kest is confident and clean.
- **Why it might not.** Coined names ask the user to trust the brand
  before it's earned their trust. Needs marketing muscle to make the mark
  mean something. No inherent semantic hook.
- **In a CTA.** "Kest built your session." — reads native, sounds like a
  proper noun. "The Kest of you." — puns on "best," which is a hazard.
- **Domain read.** kest.com likely a legal firm somewhere; kest.fit / kest.app
  likely open. Coined names generally have better domain math.

---

## Top 3 shortlist

1. **Hone** — Because the CTA writes itself and the audience already uses the
   verb. Phonetically it's not the sharpest of the fifteen, but it's the
   most semantically native replacement for Terav. The founder's chisel-T
   monogram maps cleanly to a chisel-H, and the "sharpen the plan" verb
   survives with almost no rewriting. The domain fight is real but
   `hone.app` / `hone.fit` / `usehone.com` are all live paths.

2. **Anvil** — Because it is the most confidence-forward name in the set
   on pure ear. Trochee, hard consonants front and back, clean in every
   target language, and the metaphor (your data is the surface you shape
   yourself against) is quietly correct for an adaptive engine. It doesn't
   share Terav's "sharpen" verb — it shifts the metaphor from blade to
   forge — but the shift is upward: heavier, more permanent, more
   equipment-feeling. Box test wins outright.

3. **Nock** — Because it is the most PRECISE-feeling name of the fifteen,
   and precision is the actual product. Opens soft, closes hard, and the
   metaphor (align, then release) is exactly the pre-session interaction the
   app runs every day. Long-shot on recognition — but that's what makes it
   ownable. If the founder is willing to spend one sentence teaching the
   metaphor ("nock is the notch on an arrow that seats against the string")
   this is the highest-ceiling name in the deck.

The three I cut with regret: **Kilter** (Kilter Board collision), **Whet**
(bland "wet" association), **Kest** (great phonetics, no semantic anchor —
would need a bigger budget than a founder-project has).

---

## If I were writing the cheque today

I'd rename it **Hone**. Phonetics aren't its strongest suit — Anvil and Nock
both punch harder on the ear — but the verb is already in the audience's
mouth and it makes the whole product easier to talk about. The CTA
"Sharpen the plan tonight" barely changes: it becomes "Hone tonight's
session." The founder loses the T monogram but gets a live English verb that
covers the entire product thesis in one word, and the whole "Estonian for
sharp" preamble goes away. Anvil is the more distinctive brand and I'd keep
it as a strong second if `hone` domains prove unworkable; Nock is what I'd
pick if the founder wants a name to grow into rather than a name to adopt.
