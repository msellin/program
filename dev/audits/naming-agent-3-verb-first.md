# Naming — Agent 3, Verb-First Angle

The brief in one line: the user does an action every day, so the name should BE that action. Not "log to Strava" (two words, a preposition, dead) — but "I honed today" (one verb, one word, alive). This is how Zoom, Slack, and Notion escaped noun-jail.

Every candidate below has to survive four tests:

1. **CTA test** — "___ the plan tonight." reads clean.
2. **Imperative test** — the word alone on a button tells the user what to do.
3. **Tense test** — works in "I ___ed / I'm ___ing / I'll ___ tonight."
4. **Trademark risk** — common English verbs get squatted; noted where obvious.

Terav means "sharp" in Estonian, and the founder-side verb is already **sharpen**. That's the ghost every candidate here is chasing.

---

## Candidates

### 1. Hone

- **Meaning:** To sharpen a blade against a whetstone. Metaphorically, to refine a skill with narrow, deliberate practice.
- **Fit:** Rehab and personalisation are literally hone work — one weakness at a time, session after session. The word already means what the product does; no translation needed.
- **Doesn't fit:** Slightly literary. Not a word most 25-year-old lifters would reach for unprompted, though they'd all recognise it.
- **CTA:** "Hone the plan tonight." — clean. Button: "HONE" — reads as a call to action, not a noun.
- **Tense:** "I honed today." / "I'm honing." / "I'll hone tonight." — all three work.
- **Availability read:** hone.com almost certainly taken; hone.app / hone.fit / hone.training plausible. There's a "Hone Health" (men's hormones brand) which is a real overlap flag, and a "Hone" leadership coaching platform. **Trademark risk: medium.**

### 2. Whet

- **Meaning:** To sharpen (a blade); to stimulate (an appetite). Same root as whetstone.
- **Fit:** Even tighter to the sharpen metaphor than Hone. Shorter, punchier, more physical — you hear the stone. Feels earned by a strength audience.
- **Doesn't fit:** Obscure. Non-native English speakers will say "wet." Homophone with "wet" is a real problem for a fitness brand — the last thing you want is a wet joke.
- **CTA:** "Whet the plan tonight." — interesting but readers stumble.
- **Tense:** "I whetted today" — awkward. "I whet" — sounds like a typo. Fails the tense test.
- **Availability read:** whet.app / whet.fit likely available. **Trademark risk: low but pronunciation risk high.**

### 3. Notch

- **Meaning:** A small cut in a surface. To notch = to record a win, to advance one step.
- **Fit:** Every session is a notch on the belt. Maps perfectly to the "one weak point, one session, one small gain" cadence of the product. Progressive by nature.
- **Doesn't fit:** Notch also means "meh" in slang ("top notch" vs "notch"). Less about sharpening, more about counting — subtle drift from the sharpen metaphor.
- **CTA:** "Notch the plan tonight." — reads like a to-do checkoff. Actually good for a training log.
- **Tense:** "I notched today." / "I'm notching." / "I'll notch tonight." — all three fine, present a little stiff.
- **Availability read:** notch.com very likely taken; there's Markus "Notch" Persson (Minecraft) and Notch Interfaces (Berlin motion-capture). **Trademark risk: high on .com; workable on .app/.fit.**

### 4. Forge

- **Meaning:** A blacksmith's fire; to shape metal by heat and force.
- **Fit:** Strong, tactile, physical — matches the CrossFit-and-strength audience aesthetic (barbell, sweat, iron). "Forge yourself" is already gym-poster idiom.
- **Doesn't fit:** Heavy and slightly overused in fitness (multiple gyms named Forge, protein brands, apparel). Also "forge" = counterfeit, unwelcome connotation. Less about the delicate personalisation angle, more about brute force.
- **CTA:** "Forge the plan tonight." — works. Button: "FORGE" — reads like a challenge.
- **Tense:** "I forged today." / "I'm forging." — all three work.
- **Availability read:** forge.com / forge.app almost certainly taken (Salesforce Forge, GitHub Forge patterns, many gyms). **Trademark risk: high.**

### 5. Grind

- **Meaning:** To grind a blade; to grind reps. Both meanings alive in the audience.
- **Fit:** Double duty — the tool-sharpening verb AND the athletic verb overlap for the first time in this list. "Grind" is native gym vocabulary.
- **Doesn't fit:** "Grind culture" is a tired trope, and the product's actual pitch is the OPPOSITE of grind — it's about doing LESS but SMARTER. Wrong signal.
- **CTA:** "Grind the plan tonight." — reads as bad-advice-CrossFit.
- **Tense:** All three tenses work fluently.
- **Availability read:** grind.com / grind.app taken. **Trademark risk: very high, and brand-misalignment risk higher.**

### 6. Edge

- **Meaning:** The sharp side of a blade. To edge = to sharpen, or to gain advantage.
- **Fit:** "Your edge" is exactly what the product finds and drills. Doubles as noun ("what's your edge?") and verb ("edge the plan").
- **Doesn't fit:** Overused in tech and fitness (Microsoft Edge, F45 Edge, Edge Fitness chain). Verb usage of "edge" has an NSFW slang meaning now — non-trivial issue for a young-adult audience.
- **CTA:** "Edge the plan tonight." — reads slightly off because of the slang.
- **Tense:** "I edged today." — slang problem confirmed. Fails.
- **Availability read:** edge.com taken by everything. **Trademark risk: very high, slang risk fatal.**

### 7. Prime

- **Meaning:** To prepare, to bring to peak condition (also prime numbers, primary).
- **Fit:** "Prime the session" is native prep language for athletes. Warm-up is priming. The daily action of the app IS priming the next session.
- **Doesn't fit:** Amazon Prime dominates the word. Also Optimum Nutrition Prime, Prime Hydration (Logan Paul), and every third supplement brand.
- **CTA:** "Prime the plan tonight." — reads clean.
- **Tense:** "I primed today." / "I'm priming." / "I'll prime tonight." — all three good.
- **Availability read:** every domain permutation of prime is gone. **Trademark risk: catastrophic.** Includes for completeness only.

### 8. Tune

- **Meaning:** To adjust an instrument (or engine) toward its ideal state.
- **Fit:** "Tuning" is the exact word coaches use for post-session micro-adjustments. Maps perfectly to the confirm-first adaptive-engine flow — engine proposes a tune, user accepts.
- **Doesn't fit:** Massively taken (iTunes, Tunein, Tune-based lots of SaaS). "Tune the plan" is fine, but the noun "a tune" is a song — confuses.
- **CTA:** "Tune the plan tonight." — reads clean.
- **Tense:** All three tenses work.
- **Availability read:** tune.com and tune.fm etc all gone. **Trademark risk: catastrophic.**

### 9. Drill

- **Meaning:** To rehearse a skill; a boring tool; military practice.
- **Fit:** Gym-native word. Skill blocks and rehab both call their content "drills." Every session IS a drill.
- **Doesn't fit:** "Drill" in the app-name context reads as either military or as boring-repetitive. Doesn't carry the personalisation angle.
- **CTA:** "Drill the plan tonight." — works but sounds punishing.
- **Tense:** All three tenses fine.
- **Availability read:** drill.com taken (Drill/Drill Music, Drill CRM). **Trademark risk: high.**

### 10. Log

- **Meaning:** The base action of every training app: log the set.
- **Fit:** The most honest possible name. Founder's original product was a training log. Log is what the user does every session, and it's the shortest possible verb.
- **Doesn't fit:** So generic it disappears. Also, "Log" as a brand name reads like a placeholder — every other app also lets you log. There's no personalisation angle in the name.
- **CTA:** "Log the plan tonight." — reads as boilerplate.
- **Tense:** "I logged today." — trivially true for any app.
- **Availability read:** log.com and everything log-based taken forever. **Trademark risk: n/a, brand-differentiation risk fatal.** Included for the honest counter-example.

### 11. Sharp

- **Meaning:** The English adjective Terav translates to. Also a verb-adjacent ("sharp up," "look sharp").
- **Fit:** Preserves the entire Terav concept in English, keeps the icon monogram (S instead of T, or dual-brand for a while), preserves "sharpen the plan." "Get Sharp" as a tagline writes itself.
- **Doesn't fit:** Sharp is a massive electronics brand (Sharp Corporation, Sharp TVs) and a common trademark. Also common English word — SEO nightmare. Verb-usage is weaker than Sharpen — you don't quite "sharp" something.
- **CTA:** "Sharp the plan tonight." — reads as broken English. "Sharpen" works, "Sharp" doesn't quite.
- **Tense:** "I sharped." — no. Fails.
- **Availability read:** sharp.com is Sharp Electronics. **Trademark risk: fatal.**

### 12. Sharpen

- **Meaning:** The direct translation of Terav's central verb.
- **Fit:** The internal team already uses this word. Every piece of copy, every CTA, every promise of the product is built around it. Naming the app what it does is the honest move.
- **Doesn't fit:** Two syllables and three consonant-clusters — harder to shout, harder to type, less memorable than one-syllable options. Not obviously a brand — reads as a feature name.
- **CTA:** "Sharpen the plan tonight." — the cleanest CTA in this document.
- **Tense:** "I sharpened today." / "I'm sharpening." / "I'll sharpen tonight." — all three natural.
- **Availability read:** sharpen.com taken (Sharpen contact-center software). sharpen.app / sharpen.fit possibly available. **Trademark risk: medium — noted overlap but different category.**

### 13. Etch

- **Meaning:** To cut a permanent mark into a surface with acid or a tool.
- **Fit:** Etch = to make a permanent record. Every session etches into the history. Physical, tactile, precise — same family as sharpen.
- **Doesn't fit:** Slightly more static than sharpen — you etch once, not every day. Less about the ongoing adjustment loop.
- **CTA:** "Etch the plan tonight." — reads odd; you don't etch a plan.
- **Tense:** "I etched today." / "I'm etching." — works but the semantic doesn't fit daily use.
- **Availability read:** etch.com likely taken (Etch web dev). etch.app / etch.fit plausible. **Trademark risk: medium.**

### 14. Notch (variant: Notchd / invented)

- **Invented:** "Notchd" — past-tense-as-noun style (like Grindr, Tumblr).
- **Fit:** Cleans up the trademark problem on "Notch" while keeping the checkoff semantic. Reads modern.
- **Doesn't fit:** Invented-with-a-dropped-vowel is exactly what the brief said to avoid ("tech-VC syllable smashups").
- **CTA:** "Notchd the plan tonight." — no, doesn't work as a verb form.
- **Availability read:** likely open. **Trademark risk: low, but concept doesn't survive the brief.**

### 15. Whetstone (variant: single word, noun-as-brand)

- **Meaning:** The object you sharpen a blade against. The app is the stone; the user is the blade.
- **Fit:** Physical, honest, cousin to Terav. Positions the app as tool-not-hero. Great tagline space: "Every session, against the stone."
- **Doesn't fit:** Not a verb — fails the entire angle of this agent. Two syllables. But worth including as the closest noun that expresses what Terav does.
- **CTA:** "Whetstone your plan tonight." — reads clumsy.
- **Tense:** Not a verb, so fails all tests.
- **Availability read:** whetstone.com almost certainly taken (Whetstone Education). **Trademark risk: high.** Included as the shape of the metaphor, not a serious candidate.

## Shortlist — top 3

### 1. Hone

The strongest candidate by every test. Passes CTA cleanly ("Hone the plan tonight"), passes imperative ("HONE" on a button reads as a call to action), passes all three tenses, and — critically — the word IS what the product does. Rehab is hone work. Personalisation is hone work. The brand doesn't need translation because the verb explains the product in one syllable. Trademark risk is real (Hone Health, Hone leadership) but categories are distinct enough to survive with good positioning. Monogram H is workable — not as distinctive as T, but a chiselled H or a whetstone-groove H is a real icon direction.

### 2. Sharpen

The honest name. Everything the internal team writes about the product already uses this verb. Two syllables costs you shoutability on the box wall, but wins you an immediate, transparent brand promise: "we sharpen you." Every CTA writes itself. Every tagline writes itself. The founder already thinks in this verb. The risk is that "Sharpen" doesn't feel like a brand — it feels like a feature — but the counter-example is Notion, which also names itself after a generic English noun and won. If Hone is blocked, Sharpen is next.

### 3. Notch

The dark-horse pick. It's the only candidate that reads as both a training-log verb AND a progress verb — you notch a session (log it) and you notch a PR (advance). The button "NOTCH" reads as an action. "Notched today" reads as a small, honest, unglamorous win — which is the exact tone the brief asks for (evidence over hype, honesty over marketing). Weaknesses: trademark contention on .com and the sharpen-metaphor is stretched. But it survives all four tests, and it's the most distinctive brand-shape in the shortlist.

The three dropped because: Whet fails the tense test and has a homophone problem. Forge and Grind carry wrong-tone connotations (brute force, hustle culture) for an evidence-based personalisation product. Edge fails on slang. Prime, Tune, Sharp, Log are trademark-blocked. Etch and Whetstone are semantically off or aren't verbs. Sharpen made the shortlist despite two syllables because the internal alignment is too strong to ignore.

---

## If I were writing the cheque today

**Hone.** It's the shortest possible expression of what the product does, it works as a verb in every tense the marketing copy needs, it puts a physical-craft metaphor in the user's mouth every time they open the app, and it survives the box-wall test ("I'm on Hone this cycle" reads clean). The trademark risk with Hone Health is real but manageable — different category, different colour, different audience, and a chisel-stroke monogram gives immediate visual distance from a men's-hormones brand. Buy hone.fit or hone.app, register the mark in fitness-software Class 9/42, and ship. If Hone's IP due-diligence comes back blocked, fall back to Sharpen and lean into the two-syllable literality — that's the founder's word already, and honest is the tone the audience is buying.
